**Bottom line first**: Contract development and SES (system engineering service) amount in reality to worker dispatch if the client gives direct orders to the engineer, even when the contract is named a "contract for work" or "quasi-mandate"; without a license this becomes "disguised contracting." The dividing line is set by Ministry of Labour Notice No. 37 of 1986: to be a contract for work (or quasi-mandate), the contractor must itself direct its own workers (managing their working hours and the conduct of the work) and process the work independently as its own business. Where both are not met, the party is treated as running a worker dispatch business even if the form is a contract. Preparing the text of the contract itself is for the lawyer and administrative scrivener, and corrective guidance is for the Labour Bureau; this article is general information.

## What is the difference between SES (quasi-mandate), worker dispatch, and a contract for work?

Even in the same form of "an engineer working at a client's site," the legal framework splits into three. The difference is "who gives the orders" and "what the party is responsible for."

SES is generally a quasi-mandate under the Civil Code (Articles 656 and 643): performing certain work (provision of services) with the care of a good manager. A contract for work (Civil Code Article 632) pays remuneration for the completion of work (the deliverable). In both, the one who directs the workers is the contractor (the employer), not the client. By contrast, worker dispatch means having one's own employed workers work for another, under the employment relationship, receiving that other's orders (Worker Dispatch Act Article 2, item 1). In other words, only in worker dispatch may the client give direct orders, and for that the dispatching agency needs a license.

| Framework | Who gives orders | Main basis | License of the client |
|---|---|---|---|
| Quasi-mandate (SES) | The contractor (your own company) | Civil Code Articles 656 and 643 | Not needed (but giving orders is disguised contracting) |
| Contract for work | The contractor (your own company) | Civil Code Article 632 | Not needed (same as above) |
| Worker dispatch | The client (dispatch destination) | Worker Dispatch Act Article 2, item 1 | The agency needs a license |

For the client to give orders lawfully as a worker dispatch, the dispatching agency must hold the license of the Minister of Health, Labour and Welfare. Those license requirements are set out in [getting a worker dispatch license (asset requirements, responsible person, disclosure)](/en/labor/column/rodosha-haken-jigyo-kyoka-shisan-youken-hakenmoto-sekininsha-roumu). The problem of "worker status," where a worker in general outsourcing is treated as the client's worker, is covered in [the line between outsourcing and employment (worker status)](/en/labor/column/gaichu-koyo-sakaime-roudoushasei).

## How far must the chain of command reach at the client's site before it is disguised contracting?

Being stationed at a client's site is not in itself illegal. The problem is whether the client at the destination directly instructs the engineer on how to do the work and on working hours.

Even where the contract is named a contract for work or a quasi-mandate, if there is a command relationship between the client and the worker, the reality amounts to worker dispatch, and without the agency's license it becomes unlicensed worker dispatch (disguised contracting). For example, the client's staff directly instructing the engineer on the allocation and steps of the work, the client deciding start/end times and overtime, or folding the engineer into morning meetings or shifts, are signs that the command has shifted to the client. Conversely, if the contractor places a responsible person (a manager) at the site and instructions flow through that person, and the contractor manages the working hours and the conduct of the work, it can stand as a quasi-mandate or contract for work.

| Sign | Operation leaning to disguised contracting | Operation that stands as quasi-mandate / contract |
|---|---|---|
| Work instructions | The client directly instructs the engineer | Instructions flow through the contractor's responsible person |
| Working hours | The client decides start/end times and overtime | The contractor manages working hours |
| Staffing / assignment | The client decides the assignment of people | The contractor decides staffing and assignment |
| Equipment / materials | Only using the client's for free | Some part procured at one's own responsibility and expense |

The core is that judgment turns not on "whether stationed" but on "who has the command." Naming the contract a contract for work does not change that it is judged by reality.

## How do you apply Notice No. 37 (the distinction criteria) to SES?

The line between worker dispatch and a contract for work is set by Ministry of Labour Notice No. 37 of 1986, "Criteria for Distinguishing a Worker Dispatching Business from a Business Conducted by Contract for Work." You apply this notice directly to SES and contract development.

Article 2 of that notice provides that even a business operator that has its own employed workers engage in work under a contract in the form of a contract for work is treated as running a worker dispatch business unless it falls under both 1 and 2 below. For SES to say it is "a contract for work or quasi-mandate," the contractor must meet both of these.

| Requirement of Article 2 of Notice No. 37 | Details (applied to SES) |
|---|---|
| 1 Directly using the labor of its own employed workers itself | (i) the contractor itself directs and evaluates how the work is conducted; (ii) it itself directs and manages start/end times, breaks, days off, and overtime; (iii) it itself directs and manages matters such as maintaining order in the enterprise |
| 2 Processing the contracted work independently as its own business | (i) procuring and paying the funds for processing the work at its own responsibility; (ii) bearing the responsibilities of a business operator under the Civil Code and the Commercial Code; (iii) not merely providing physical labor (procuring the machinery, equipment, and materials at its own responsibility and expense, or processing the work based on its own planning or its own expert skills or experience) |

Further, Article 3 of that notice provides that even where a party formally falls under the items of Article 2, if that is deliberately disguised to evade the application of the Act and the true purpose is to carry out worker dispatch as a business, the party cannot escape being one that runs a worker dispatch business. Arranging the contract alone is not enough; the operation at the site must follow the notice. Concrete applications are given in the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare's Q&A collection (volumes 1 and 2) (referenced 26 November 2026).

## What should the client and the contractor each put in place?

The key to avoiding disguised contracting is to keep the command and the working-hours management fully on the contractor's side. The points to put in place differ between the client and the contractor.

The contractor (the SES / contract-development side) places a responsible person at the site to unify instructions, manages working hours in-house, and makes the scope of work and the deliverables clear in the contract. The client (the receiving side) does not give the engineer direct work or overtime instructions, and runs things so that requests go to the contractor's responsible person. If the client does want to give direct orders, the proper route is to receive the worker not as a contract for work or quasi-mandate but as a worker dispatch from a licensed agency.

| Party | What to put in place |
|---|---|
| Contractor (SES / contract development) | Placing a site responsible person and unifying instructions / managing working hours and attendance in-house / making the scope of work, deliverables, and acceptance criteria clear / preparing work rules and employment management |
| Client (receiving side) | Running things without direct instructions to the engineer / routing requests through the contractor's responsible person / receiving as a licensed dispatch if direct command is needed |
| Text of the contract | Preparing the business consignment contract or quasi-mandate contract is the realm of the lawyer and administrative scrivener |

Preparing the text of the contract itself is the work of the lawyer and administrative scrivener, and building the work rules, employment management, and working-hours system is the realm of the Certified Social Insurance and Labor Consultant. Where these cross business entities, each is contracted separately as an independent entity, and there is no referral fee. ## What happens if you are flagged for disguised contracting?

Disguised contracting is subject to corrective guidance as unlicensed worker dispatch, and an unexpected effect reaches the client side too. Simply switching the contract may not settle it.

If the Labour Bureau judges that the reality amounts to worker dispatch, it gives corrective guidance to both the client and the contractor. Carrying out worker dispatch as a business without a license violates the Worker Dispatch Act and can be subject to penalties (Article 59 and so on). Further, where dispatched workers were received in a state of disguised contracting and the like, the labor-contract-offer-deemed system (Article 40-6 of that Act) may deem the client (dispatch destination) to have offered a labor contract to that dispatched worker on the same working conditions. This is a heavy effect for the client.

| What can happen if flagged | Basis |
|---|---|
| Corrective guidance by the Labour Bureau (both client and contractor) | Guidance and advice under the Worker Dispatch Act |
| Subject to penalties as unlicensed dispatch | Worker Dispatch Act Article 59 and so on |
| Deemed labor-contract offer (the client is deemed to have offered) | Worker Dispatch Act Article 40-6 |

Corrective guidance itself is the Labour Bureau's authority, and individual disputes (such as damages or confirmation of status) are the realm of the lawyer. This article is general information, and whether a particular case amounts to disguised contracting must be confirmed against the reality of the operation at the site. Because judgment turns on the operation rather than the form of the contract, it helps to design the flow of command and working-hours management before concluding the contract.

## Frequently asked questions

**Q. If I name the contract a "quasi-mandate," is it not disguised contracting even with site stationing?**
A. You cannot say it is not. Whether it is disguised contracting is judged not by the contract's name but by the reality of the operation. Ministry of Labour Notice No. 37 of 1986 provides that even in the form of a contract for work, a party is treated as running a worker dispatch business unless the contractor itself directs its own workers (managing the conduct of the work and working hours) and processes the work independently as its own business. Even with a quasi-mandate contract, if the client directly gives the engineer work or overtime instructions, the reality can be judged to be worker dispatch (disguised contracting if unlicensed).

**Q. What is the problem if the client gives direct instructions to the engineer?**
A. Direct instruction is a sign that command has shifted to the client, leaning toward worker dispatch rather than a contract for work or quasi-mandate. If it is a worker dispatch received from a licensed agency, the command is lawful, but giving direct orders in the form of a contract for work or quasi-mandate can become unlicensed worker dispatch (disguised contracting). Route requests through the contractor's site responsible person, or, if direct command is needed, receive the worker as a licensed dispatch.

**Q. What effect does a finding of disguised contracting have on the client?**
A. Besides corrective guidance from the Labour Bureau, the labor-contract-offer-deemed system (Worker Dispatch Act Article 40-6) may deem the client (dispatch destination) to have offered a labor contract on the same working conditions to a dispatched worker received in a state of disguised contracting and the like. Unlicensed dispatch can also be subject to penalties (Article 59 and so on). Switching the contract may not settle it, and you may need to review the operation during receipt.
