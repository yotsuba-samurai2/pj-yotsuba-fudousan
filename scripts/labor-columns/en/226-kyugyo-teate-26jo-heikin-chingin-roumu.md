**Bottom line first**: When you put workers on leave for a reason attributable to the employer, you must pay a leave allowance of at least 60% of average wages during the leave (Labour Standards Act Article 26). "A reason attributable to the employer" covers all cases where the employer cannot claim force majeure — shortage of materials, a supervisory authority's recommendation to suspend, management-side obstacles — and is a broader concept than the attributability under Civil Code Article 536(2) (Northwest Airlines case, Supreme Court Second Petty Bench, 17 July 1987). Force majeure that removes the duty applies only where both hold: the cause arose outside the business, and it could not be avoided even with the utmost care of an ordinary manager. Average wages are, as a rule, the total wages over the preceding three months divided by the total number of days (Article 12). Whenever demand falls, equipment breaks down, or an outbreak stops operations, the question is always how much to pay for the days workers are rested. Even under "no work, no pay", when the cause lies with the employer a duty to pay a leave allowance arises. This page focuses on the area a Shakai Hoken Roumushi handles: calculating the Article 26 leave allowance, the line with force majeure, and the difference from Civil Code Article 536(2). Representation in disputes over unpaid amounts is an attorney's area; here we focus on designing the allowance.

## What is the leave allowance (Article 26, at least 60% of average wages)?

The leave allowance is paid during a leave imposed for a reason attributable to the employer. Article 26 requires the employer to pay at least 60% of average wages during the leave. It is a mandatory rule to secure a minimum of the worker's living, and non-payment can draw penalties (the fine under Article 120) and an additional payment (Article 114).

| Item | Content | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| When the duty arises | Leave for a reason attributable to the employer | Labour Standards Act Article 26 |
| Amount | At least 60% of average wages | Labour Standards Act Article 26 |
| Average-wage calculation | As a rule, total wages over the preceding three months ÷ total days | Labour Standards Act Article 12 |
| Consequence of non-payment | Fine (Article 120), additional payment (Article 114), delay damages | Each article |

Because it is "at least 60%", setting a higher rate by work rules or agreement is unobjectionable. Where you review wages or work along with a leave, preparing work rules is a precondition. See [from how many employees are work rules required](/en/labor/column/shugyokisoku-10nin-gimu-nani-ga-hitsuyo).

## What is "a reason attributable to the employer", and how is force majeure judged?

"A reason attributable to the employer" in Article 26 is not limited to the employer's intent or negligence. The administrative reading takes it as any reason within the employer's managerial or operational responsibility where the employer cannot claim force majeure — a wider scope than fault liability in civil law.

| Example | How to view the duty |
|---|---|
| Leave from falling orders / poor business | A management obstacle may be a reason attributable to the employer (duty to pay) |
| Shortage of materials or fuel; suspension on a supervisory authority's recommendation | May be included in reasons attributable to the employer |
| Machine breakdown / inspection | A managerial reason may trigger the duty |
| Natural disaster and other force majeure | No duty if the two requirements are met (below) |

Force majeure that removes the duty applies only where both of the following hold.

| Force-majeure requirement | Content |
|---|---|
| (1) External cause | The cause arose as an accident outside the business |
| (2) Unavoidable | It could not be avoided even with the utmost care of an ordinary manager |

For instance, a client's circumstances, a parent company's difficulty, or a rise in raw-material prices may look external, but whether alternatives were secured and leave-avoidance efforts exhausted is asked. If (2) is not met, it is not force majeure and may be subject to the leave allowance. The final judgement on whether a given case is force majeure is made by a qualified professional after a consultation.

## How does the leave allowance differ from Civil Code Article 536(2) (full-wage claim)?

When the cause of the leave lies with the employer, the worker may claim full wages (100%) under Civil Code Article 536(2). It differs in both basis and amount from the Article 26 leave allowance (at least 60%).

| | Labour Standards Act Article 26 (leave allowance) | Civil Code Article 536(2) (counter-performance) |
|---|---|---|
| Nature | Mandatory, penalty-backed; secures a minimum 60% | Default rule; full (100%) claim possible where attributable |
| Scope of attributability | Broad (covers all cases where force majeure cannot be claimed) | Narrow (the creditor = employer's intent or negligence, etc.) |
| Typical | Duty arises even for management/operational obstacles | Leave/dismissal where the employer's attributability is found |

The Supreme Court held that Article 26 does not exclude the application of Civil Code Article 536(2), and that where the cause of leave also meets the attributability under Article 536(2), the leave-allowance claim and the wage claim may overlap. It held that "a reason attributable to the employer" in Article 26 is broader than the attributability under Article 536(2) (Northwest Airlines case, Supreme Court Second Petty Bench, 17 July 1987). The Supreme Court has also held that interim income the worker earned elsewhere during a dismissal period due to the employer's attributability may be deducted from wages only from the portion exceeding 60% of average wages, given the Article 26 aim of securing a minimum 60% (Akebono Taxi case, Supreme Court First Petty Bench, 2 April 1987). In short, where the employer is at fault, 60% may not suffice and full wages or the difference may be contested. The final judgement on the amount is made by a qualified professional.

## How do you calculate average wages (Article 12, the preceding three months)?

Average wages are, as a rule, the total wages paid in the three months before the day the ground for calculation arose, divided by the total number of days (calendar days) in that period (Article 12). Where there is a wage closing date, you count from the immediately preceding closing date.

| Category | Calculation |
|---|---|
| Principle | Total wages of the preceding three months ÷ total days (calendar days) |
| Minimum guarantee (daily, hourly, piece-rate, etc.) | Total wages of the preceding three months ÷ actual working days × 60% (whichever is higher against the principle amount) |
| Excluded from the wage total | Days and wages for work injury, pre/post-natal, childcare/family-care leave, probation, etc.; wages paid extraordinarily; wages paid at intervals exceeding three months (bonuses, etc.) |

The leave allowance is at least 60% of this average wage. Note that average wages are a separate mechanism from the "standard monthly remuneration" used to compute social-insurance premiums. See [the difference between the base-calculation notification and the monthly-change notification](/en/labor/column/santei-kiso-getsu-gaku-henko-chigai).

## How are partial leave, half-day leave and holidays treated?

The Article 26 approach works even for a "partial leave" resting only part of a day. The administrative reading says that where the wages paid for the time actually worked fall short of 60% of average wages, you pay the difference as a leave allowance.

| Case | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Half-day / partial leave | If the wages for the hours actually worked fall short of 60% of average wages, pay the difference as a leave allowance |
| A holiday with no work obligation | Not subject to the leave allowance (a day with no work and no wages) |
| A scheduled day off under variable hours | A day with no work obligation is outside; leave on a working day is subject to Article 26 |

If you use variable working hours to match the busy and slack pattern, which days are working days and which are off changes whether the leave allowance is due. See [choosing variable working hours and flextime](/en/labor/column/henkei-roudoujikan-flextime-erabikata). Where you use fixed overtime pay, how to treat the premium portion during leave also needs organising (see [the legal requirements for fixed overtime pay and difference settlement](/en/labor/column/kotei-zangyodai-tekiho-youken-sagaku-seisan)).

## Frequently asked questions

**Q. Orders fell and we rested staff for a few days. Can we skip wages?**
A. Leave from falling orders or poor business may be a management obstacle = "a reason attributable to the employer" (Article 26). In that case you must pay a leave allowance of at least 60% of average wages during the leave. Where the employer's attributability is strong, full wages may also be claimed under Civil Code Article 536(2). The individual judgement is made by a qualified professional.

**Q. Is a leave allowance needed when we close for a typhoon or earthquake?**
A. There is no duty if it is force majeure, but the test is strict. Both must hold: (1) the cause arose as an accident outside the business, and (2) it could not be avoided even with the utmost care of an ordinary manager. If leave-avoidance efforts (alternatives, keeping part of the work going) were not exhausted, it is not recognised as force majeure and a duty may arise.

**Q. Is paying 60% always enough for the leave allowance?**
A. The minimum Article 26 secures is 60% of average wages, but where the employer's attributability for the cause is found, full wages may be claimed under Civil Code Article 536(2). The Supreme Court (Akebono Taxi case, 2 April 1987) held that interim income may be deducted only from the portion exceeding 60% of average wages. Whether 60% suffices depends on the case.

**Q. What if we rested a worker only for half a day?**
A. Even in a partial leave, where the wages for the hours actually worked fall short of 60% of average wages, you pay the difference as a leave allowance. A holiday with no work obligation is not subject to the leave allowance. Under variable working hours, whether the day is a working day or off changes the outcome, so organise it along the work calendar.
