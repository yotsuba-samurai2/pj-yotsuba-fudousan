**In short:** Taxi drivers' working hours are managed under the Labor Standards Act plus the **Improvement Standards Notice** (Standards for Improving the Working Hours, etc., of Motor Vehicle Drivers). Under the current standard (amended December 23, 2022 (Reiwa 4), **applied from April 1, 2024 (Reiwa 6)**), **alternating-day work** (kakujitsu kinmu, a single shift spanning two calendar days) has on-duty hours of **up to 22 hours over the two days (and 21 hours on average per shift across two shifts), and up to 262 hours a month** (270 for up to 6 months a year under a labour-management agreement), with a rest period that is **based on a continuous 24 hours after duty and does not fall below a continuous 22 hours**. Premium wages are required even on commission pay. This page sets out what changed in 2024, how to calculate alternating-day work, premium wages on commission pay, how standby and naps are treated, and where to fix the work rules and the 36 Agreement.

Taxi work has two features — long on-duty stretches that span day and night, and commission pay linked to takings — so labour management designed for other industries does not transfer as-is. This page is for taxi operators and the labour staff who handle dispatch and operation management. From the viewpoint of a shakai hoken roumushi's labour management, it sets out the 2024 amendment to the Improvement Standards Notice, the on-duty hours and rest periods of alternating-day work, premium wages that are due even on commission pay, the treatment of standby and naps, and where to fix the work rules and the 36 Agreement.

## What changed in the 2024 version of taxi drivers' Improvement Standards Notice?

The Improvement Standards Notice is a notice of the Minister of Health, Labour and Welfare that sets upper and lower limits on "**on-duty hours**" (working hours plus breaks and standby time, from start to finish of duty) and on the "**rest period**" between shifts. It was amended on December 23, 2022 (Reiwa 4) and has applied since April 1, 2024 (Reiwa 6). From the same April 1, 2024, the **overtime cap** also applies to motor-vehicle driving (up to 960 hours a year even under a special clause; Labor Standards Act, Supplementary Provisions, Article 140). The "overtime cap" and the "Improvement Standards Notice" are different yardsticks, and **you must satisfy both at the same time**.

Taxi (hire and taxi) drivers have two working patterns — "**day-shift work**," worked day by day, and "**alternating-day work**," in which two calendar days (roughly two days' worth) form a single shift worked every other day — and on-duty standards are set separately for each. The main changes for day-shift work are as follows.

| Item | Before (through March 31, 2024) | Current (from April 1, 2024) |
|---|---|---|
| On-duty hours per month (day shift) | 299 hours | 288 hours |
| On-duty hours per day (day shift) | 13 hours (up to 16) | 13 hours in principle, up to 15 (exceeding 14 hours: a guideline of up to 3 times a week) |
| Rest period (day shift) | A continuous 8 hours or more | Based on a continuous 11 hours, not below 9 |

For how trucking changed under the same 2024 problem, see [The "2024 problem" in trucking and the Improvement Standards Notice](/en/labor/column/unso-2024-mondai-kaizen-kijun-kokuji). Note that the on-duty figures differ by sector.

## How do you calculate on-duty hours and rest periods for alternating-day work (two calendar days)?

Alternating-day work bundles two calendar days (two days' worth) into one shift, with the following day off as a post-shift rest. On-duty hours are measured not "per day" but "per two calendar days" and "per month." The current standards are as follows.

| Item | Principle | Exception / limit |
|---|---|---|
| On-duty hours per two calendar days | Within 22 hours | and no more than 21 hours on average per shift across two alternating-day shifts |
| On-duty hours per month | Within 262 hours | Up to 270 hours for up to 6 months a year under a labour-management agreement, where there are regional or other special circumstances |
| Rest period | Based on a continuous 24 hours after duty | Not below a continuous 22 hours |

For example, if one two-day shift was 21 hours 30 minutes, it is within 22 hours on its own, but because **the average of two adjacent shifts must not exceed 21 hours**, the other shift must be held to 20 hours 30 minutes or less. Meeting "within 22 hours" per shift is not enough; you must also watch the average of two consecutive shifts — this is where the calculation is easy to get wrong.

The Improvement Standards Notice is not the Labor Standards Act itself but a notice; still, a breach is subject to corrective guidance from the Labor Standards Inspection Office and can lead to transport-administration sanctions (administrative dispositions against the operator). The difference from the overtime cap is that **it is not "unlimited as long as you have an agreement."**

## Even on commission pay (progressive commission is prohibited), is overtime pay required?

It is. Even on commission pay (a piece-rate/output-based system), premium wages must be paid for overtime beyond statutory working hours, for holiday work, and for late-night work (Labor Standards Act, Article 37). Taxi work often involves late-night driving, so late-night premiums (25%+ from 10 p.m. to 5 a.m.) arise routinely.

The premium rate for commission pay is calculated a little differently from fixed pay. For an output-based or other piece-rate system, you take **the total commission paid in the wage-calculation period, divided by the total hours worked in that period** as the hourly amount, and multiply it by the premium rate (Ordinance for Enforcement of the Labor Standards Act, Article 19, paragraph 1, item 6). Where fixed pay and commission pay are combined, calculate each part separately and add them.

| Category | Overtime | Holiday | Late-night |
|---|---|---|---|
| Premium rate | 25%+ (50%+ over 60 hours a month) | 35%+ | 25%+ |

A **progressive commission system** (where commission rises in steps according to takings and the like) can extremely induce long working hours and speeding, and the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare, by circular, **instructs that it be abolished** (Rodo Kijun Kyoku circular Kihatsu No. 93 of 1989). The same circular requires that, even under a commission system, a **guaranteed wage** be set so that fixed pay and commission together secure roughly 60% or more of the ordinary wage. Commission pay is also subject to the Minimum Wage Act, so the amount converted to an hourly figure must not fall below the minimum wage (Minimum Wage Act, Article 4).

## How are breaks and naps (such as garage standby) treated?

On-duty hours under the Improvement Standards Notice include not only working hours but also breaks and the time spent waiting for customers or on standby. Whether a period is a break (not working time) or standby (working time) turns on whether the driver is under the employer's command, and it is judged on the actual situation.

There is a special rule that allows extending on-duty hours, under certain conditions, for a "**garage-standby driver**" (a working pattern of waiting at a garage or the like in response to customer demand). In alternating-day garage standby, only where both of the following are met — (1) giving a **nap of 4 hours or more at night**, and (2) the number of times the per-two-day on-duty ceiling (22 hours) is exceeded being **within 7 times a month** — can the monthly on-duty hours be extended to 262 hours (or 270 under a labour-management agreement) plus 10 hours, and the per-two-day on-duty hours to **up to 24 hours**. In day-shift garage standby, a labour-management agreement can extend the monthly on-duty hours to 300 hours.

For **unforeseeable events** such as an accident, breakdown, or disaster that cause a delay, the time required to deal with them may, only where objective records are recognised, be excluded in applying the per-day or per-two-day on-duty limits. However, if including that time would exceed the maximum on-duty hours, a rest period must be secured after duty (a continuous 11 hours or more for a one-day shift, a continuous 24 hours or more for a two-day shift). In every case, recording the reality of naps and standby through a digital tachograph or daily log is the starting point.

## How should you fix the work rules and the 36 Agreement?

Satisfying both the overtime cap and the Improvement Standards Notice requires careful drafting. The points to fix are as follows.

| What to do | Point to review |
|---|---|
| 36 Agreement (special clause) | Design overtime within 960 hours a year, and state the frequency of the special clause (up to 6 times a year), the reasons, and the procedure |
| Work rules and wage rules | State and align the day-shift / alternating-day on-duty hours and rest periods, the method of calculating commission pay, the guaranteed wage, and the calculation of premium wages |
| Variable working-hour system | Alternating-day work is commonly designed under a one-month variable working-hour system (Labor Standards Act, Article 32-2). Put the scheduled hours and the placement of days off into the rules |
| Records of on-duty and working hours | Cross-check the digital tachograph and daily logs against attendance, and run day-to-day management against the lines of 22 hours per two days, 262 hours a month, and a 24-hour rest |
| Selection of the majority representative | Select someone who is not in a managerial position through a proper procedure, and keep a record (Ordinance for Enforcement of the Labor Standards Act, Article 6-2) |

Work rules must be drawn up and filed by a workplace that regularly employs 10 or more workers (Labor Standards Act, Article 89). For how many employees make work rules mandatory, see [How many employees make work rules mandatory](/en/labor/column/shugyokisoku-10nin-gimu-nani-ga-hitsuyo), and for the basics of the 36 Agreement and the overtime ceiling, see [The 36 Agreement and the overtime ceiling](/en/labor/column/36-kyotei-zangyo-sharoushi-doko-made).

## Frequently asked questions

**Q. If the two-day on-duty hours are within 22 hours, is that enough on its own?**
A. No. Two-day on-duty hours must be "within 22 hours" and also meet "no more than 21 hours on average per shift across two alternating-day shifts." Even if each shift is within 22 hours, if the average of two consecutive shifts exceeds 21 hours it breaches the Improvement Standards Notice. You must also meet the monthly on-duty hours (262 hours in principle, 270 for up to 6 months a year under a labour-management agreement) and the rest period (based on a continuous 24 hours, not below 22).

**Q. If we set pay on commission alone, can we skip overtime pay?**
A. No. Even on commission pay (an output-based system), premium wages for overtime, holiday and late-night work are required (Labor Standards Act, Article 37). The premium rate for commission pay is found by dividing the total commission in the wage-calculation period by the total hours worked in that period (Ordinance for Enforcement of the Labor Standards Act, Article 19, paragraph 1, item 6). Also, a progressive commission system that rises in steps with takings is one the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare instructs, by circular, to abolish.

**Q. Does garage-standby or customer-waiting time count as on-duty time?**
A. Yes. On-duty hours under the Improvement Standards Notice cover the time from start to end of duty, including breaks and standby time. A garage-standby driver may extend on-duty hours where conditions such as giving a nap of 4 hours or more at night are met, but the conditions and frequency are fixed (for alternating-day work, exceeding 22 hours per two days is allowed within 7 times a month). Grasping the reality of standby and naps through records is the starting point.
