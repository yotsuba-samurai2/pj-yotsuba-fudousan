**In short:** When supporting an employee who works while treating cancer or an intractable disease, **before reducing it to a "leave or resign" choice, combine workplace measures such as shorter hours and hourly annual leave with economic support such as the injury-and-sickness allowance.** The Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare's "Guidelines for Supporting the Balance between Treatment and Work at the Workplace" (revised March 2024) set out a flow in which the employer considers workplace measures based on the attending physician's opinion and draws up and runs a balance-support plan. Hourly annual leave can be introduced within 5 days a year under a labour-management agreement (Labor Standards Act, Article 39, paragraph 4), and the injury-and-sickness allowance is paid, counted up to a total of 1 year and 6 months, while the person cannot work because of a non-work injury or illness (Health Insurance Act, Article 99). This page sets out where the company starts, how to set up the work rules, combining with the allowance, coordinating with the attending physician, and the division of roles.

This page is for the HR staff and managers of small and medium-sized enterprises where an employee wishes to keep working while treating cancer or an intractable disease. From the viewpoint of a shakai hoken roumushi's labour management, it sets out the practice of balance support. Medical judgments are the domain of the attending physician and the industrial physician; what the company does is put workplace measures and systems in place.

## Where does the company start to balance cancer treatment and work?

Start by **receiving the employee's request and confirming the attending physician's opinion (whether work can continue and the desirable workplace measures) in writing.** The company does not judge the medical condition directly; rather, it obtains medical information from the attending physician and considers workplace measures in light of it.

The Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare's "Guidelines for Supporting the Balance between Treatment and Work at the Workplace" (revised March 2024) set out roughly the following flow.

| Stage | What the company does |
|---|---|
| 1. Provision of information / request | The employee tells the attending physician about the job and obtains an opinion on whether work can continue and on points of consideration, and submits it to the company |
| 2. Collecting and considering information | Considers workplace measures (working hours, job content) in light of the attending physician's and the industrial physician's opinions |
| 3. Drawing up a balance-support plan | Makes concrete, with the employee's consent, measures such as shorter hours, hourly annual leave, or reassignment, and their period |
| 4. Implementation and follow-up | Runs the plan and reviews it as treatment progresses |

These guidelines are not a legal obligation in themselves but a guide; still, the employer has a duty to endeavour to take measures necessary to maintain and promote workers' health (Industrial Safety and Health Act, Article 69, paragraph 1). Running it as "leave first, to concentrate on treatment" alone can mean putting on leave someone who could have kept working. **The starting point is to have, as a system, the option of working while being treated.** For how to handle leave and return from mental-health problems, see also [When you receive a medical certificate for mental-health problems](/en/labor/column/mental-fucho-kyushoku-fukushoku).

## How do you put hourly annual leave and shorter hours into the work rules?

Hospital visits and dealing with side effects are often met by "a few hours off" rather than "a full day off," so **hourly annual leave and shorter hours** become the practical centre.

Hourly annual leave can be given, by concluding a labour-management agreement, **within 5 days a year** by the hour out of annual paid leave (Labor Standards Act, Article 39, paragraph 4). It suits visits for which a half-day or full-day of leave is too much. For the introduction procedure, see [Introducing hourly annual leave](/en/labor/column/jikantan-nenkyu-dounyu-roushi-kyotei-shugyokisoku).

| System | Basis / requirement | Suited to |
|---|---|---|
| Hourly annual leave | Given by the hour within 5 days a year under a labour-management agreement (Labor Standards Act, Article 39, paragraph 4) | Short visits, tests, medication |
| Shorter hours / staggered start | Designable at will if set in the work rules (not a legal obligation) | Recovery phases, consideration for side effects |
| Exemption from overtime / late-night work | Set as a measure in the work rules or the balance-support plan | Reducing the burden during treatment |
| Special leave (sick leave, etc.) | Designable at will if set in the work rules | Making up when paid leave runs short |

Shorter hours and sick leave are not legal obligations, but if set in the work rules they can be run as company systems. For the size of workplace at which drawing up and filing work rules becomes mandatory, see [How many employees make work rules mandatory](/en/labor/column/shugyokisoku-10nin-gimu-nani-ga-hitsuyo). When fitting a system into the work rules, set out together the eligible persons, period, treatment of wages, and application procedure.

## Can the injury-and-sickness allowance be combined with visits and shorter hours?

The injury-and-sickness allowance is a Health Insurance benefit paid, after 3 consecutive waiting days, from the fourth day, **when the person cannot work because of a non-work injury or illness** (Health Insurance Act, Article 99). Roughly two-thirds of the standard daily remuneration is paid per day.

With the amendment effective January 1, 2022 (Reiwa 4), the payment period became "**a total of** 1 year and 6 months from the start date of payment." Any period during which no payment was made because the person worked can be carried over and counted up. It is an amendment that made the allowance easier to use for illnesses such as cancer, where treatment and work go back and forth.

Combining with shorter hours involves an adjustment against remuneration.

| State | Treatment of the injury-and-sickness allowance |
|---|---|
| Fully unable to work, no remuneration | Paid (after 3 waiting days, up to a total of 1 year and 6 months) |
| Able to receive all or part of remuneration | In principle not paid; but if the remuneration is less than the allowance, the difference is paid (Health Insurance Act, Article 108, paragraph 1) |
| Returns once, then becomes unable to work again | May be paid again within the total of 1 year and 6 months from the start date |

In other words, working on shorter hours and earning remuneration triggers an adjustment, with a difference paid where the remuneration is less than the allowance amount. You cannot "work and also receive the full allowance," but the totalling and the difference payment make it easier to design a combination of treatment and work. For the payment requirements and the idea of totalling, see [The requirements and totalling of the injury-and-sickness allowance](/en/labor/column/shobyo-teate-kin-shikyu-yoken-tsusan).

## How do the attending physician's opinion and the company's workplace measures connect?

The axis of the connection is **the split of roles: "medical judgments to the attending physician and the industrial physician; decisions on workplace measures to the company."** The company receives the attending physician's opinion in the form of "can work continue" and "what consideration is desirable," and fits it into the reality of the job.

| Information / judgment | Who carries it |
|---|---|
| Medical judgment of the condition, treatment policy, and whether work can continue | The attending physician |
| Medical advice on workplace measures in light of the job content | The industrial physician (at workplaces required to appoint one) |
| Deciding workplace measures (working hours, job content), drawing up and running the balance-support plan | The company (HR, managers) |
| System design and procedures (work rules, applying for the allowance, etc.) | Supported by a shakai hoken roumushi |

Rather than deciding mechanically on the attending physician's "fit to return" or "fit to work" alone, the measures are set against the reality of the job. That the company handles health information with the person's consent, and reviews the plan regularly, are also points the guidelines require.

## Frequently asked questions

**Q. Can we put an employee on leave for cancer treatment without asking their wishes?**
A. Rather than immediately forcing a "leave or resign" choice, the flow set out in the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare's guidelines (revised March 2024) is to first confirm whether work can continue and the desirable consideration based on the attending physician's opinion, and to consider workplace measures such as shorter hours and hourly annual leave. Uniformly putting on leave someone who can keep working does not fit the purpose of balance support. The final legal judgment on the validity of dismissal or leave is the domain of an attorney.

**Q. How many days of hourly annual leave can be used?**
A. By concluding a labour-management agreement, up to 5 days a year of annual paid leave can be given by the hour (Labor Standards Act, Article 39, paragraph 4). It suits situations where you want to step out for just a few hours, such as a hospital visit or medication. The part beyond 5 days cannot be made hourly, so it is designed in combination with other systems such as shorter hours or special leave.

**Q. Can an employee receive the injury-and-sickness allowance while working shorter hours?**
A. The injury-and-sickness allowance is a benefit for when a person cannot work. While they can receive remuneration it is in principle not paid, but if that remuneration is less than the allowance, the difference is paid (Health Insurance Act, Article 108, paragraph 1). The payment period is a total of 1 year and 6 months from the start date, and any period with no payment because of work can be carried over and counted up (same Act, Article 99; amendment effective January 1, 2022). Whether it is paid in an individual case is the insurer's decision.

**Q. If the attending physician writes "fit to work," can the company have them work accordingly?**
A. The attending physician's opinion is important information for knowing whether work can continue and the desirable consideration, but the company decides workplace measures against the reality of the job. It is a split of roles: medical judgment of the condition to the attending physician and the industrial physician, decisions on workplace measures to the company. The company handles health information with the person's consent, and reviews the plan as treatment progresses.
