## Who is the majority representative, and what role does it decide?

The majority representative stands for the employee side in concluding labour-management agreements and giving an opinion on work rules, when the workplace has no union organising a majority of workers. Where a union holds the majority, that union, not a representative, is the counterparty.

| Situation | What the majority representative does | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| 36 agreement (overtime/holiday work) | Concludes a written agreement on the workers’ side using a method that clearly records both sides’ consent. Signatures or seals are examples; neither is required on a separate filing form. | Labour Standards Act Article 36(1) |
| Making or changing work rules | The employer hears the representative’s opinion and attaches the written opinion when filing. No signature or seal from the representative is required. | Labour Standards Act Article 90(1) and (2) |
| Other agreements (wage deduction, variable hours, planned leave, etc.) | Concludes the agreement for the employee side | Each basis provision |

As a rule, before requiring work beyond statutory hours or on a statutory day off, the employer must conclude a valid Article 36 agreement for the workplace and file it with the competent Labour Standards Inspection Office. Signing alone is not enough. Even after filing, the agreement cannot be relied on before its effective period begins. Filing work rules (duty to make and file at workplaces regularly employing 10 or more; Article 89) requires the representative's opinion paper. In both procedures the counterpart matters, but the legal consequences of a defect differ. See [how far a 36 agreement can allow overtime](/en/labor/column/36-kyotei-zangyo-sharoushi-doko-made) and [from how many employees are work rules required](/en/labor/column/shugyokisoku-10nin-gimu-nani-ga-hitsuyo).

## What is a valid selection procedure (vote, show of hands, endorsement — and why employer nomination is not allowed)?

Enforcement Rules Article 6-2(1) sets two requirements for the majority representative.

| Requirement | Content | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| (1) Position | Not a manager or supervisor under Labour Standards Act Article 41, item (ii) | Enforcement Rules Article 6-2(1)(i) |
| (2) Procedure | Chosen by a vote, show of hands or similar method that makes clear the purpose is "to select a party to the agreement", and not chosen based on the employer's intention | Enforcement Rules Article 6-2(1)(ii) |

The "etc." in "vote, show of hands, etc." is read by the authorities to include worker discussion or a circulated resolution — any democratic procedure that makes clear a majority of workers supports that person. By contrast, the employer saying "please take this person", or using the social-club head automatically, does not satisfy (2): if the employer's intention decides the representative, it cannot work as a mechanism to pool the employee side's will. You show which agreement or filing the person is being chosen for, and choose in a form in which all workers (including part-timers and managers/supervisors in the denominator) can join the vote or endorsement.

## Can a manager or supervisor be the majority representative?

No. Enforcement Rules Article 6-2(1)(i) excludes managers and supervisors from being the majority representative. A manager/supervisor is outside the working-hours rules (Article 41, item (ii)) and stands close to the employer. But manager/supervisor status is judged by substance, not title; a "manager in name only" is not a manager/supervisor. See [managers in name only and premium wages](/en/labor/column/kanri-kantokusha-nabakari-warimashi-shinya).

As an exception, where there is no one meeting item (i) — for example a workplace where everyone is a manager/supervisor — Enforcement Rules Article 6-2(2) treats, for opinion-hearing on work rules and the like, a person meeting the procedure requirement (2) as the representative. Even then it may not be decided by the employer's intention. The denominator for "majority" counts managers/supervisors too. This exception does not apply to selecting a representative for an Article 36 agreement.

## If the selection is improper, what happens to the 36 agreement or work-rule filing?

If the selection lacks the requirements, the 36 agreement the representative concluded is invalid. An invalid 36 agreement gives no legal basis to order overtime or holiday work, and ordered overtime may breach Articles 32 and 35. The Supreme Court did not recognise as a valid agreement a 36 agreement concluded by a social-club head who became the majority representative automatically, and held there is no duty to obey an overtime order based on it (Tokoro case, Supreme Court Second Petty Bench, 22 June 2001).

| Example of improper selection | Main consequence |
|---|---|
| The employer nominated the representative | 36 agreement invalid = loses the basis for overtime; work-rule consultation is procedurally defective |
| A social-club head used automatically | Same (Tokoro case) |
| A manager/supervisor made representative | Lacks requirement (1); unfit as representative |
| Chosen without showing the purpose (reusing a representative chosen for another purpose) | Lacks the stated purpose "to select a party to the agreement"; does not satisfy (2) |

For work rules, the employer must consult the workplace’s majority union or, if there is none, a properly selected majority representative, and attach the written opinion when filing. Consultation is required, not consent. A procedural defect does not automatically invalidate the rules themselves. Their effect under employment contract law is a separate question, taking account of factors such as how the rules were made known and whether the terms are reasonable. If wage deduction, variable hours, planned leave and other agreements were concluded with the same representative, their validity may be challenged together. That is why keeping a record of "who was chosen, and how" matters.

## What to watch for in electronic selection and notification?

In teleworking or multi-site workplaces, selection and notification are increasingly done electronically. Doing the selection by internal email, intranet or electronic voting is unobjectionable in itself, as long as the form is a democratic "vote, show of hands, etc." procedure meeting (2). The MHLW's Q&A also gives, as an example of the consideration owed so the representative can carry out the work smoothly (Enforcement Rules Article 6-2(4); added by MHLW Ordinance No. 112 of 2018, effective 1 April 2019), the provision of office equipment and space such as internal email and the intranet.

| Point | Practical note |
|---|---|
| Electronic selection | All workers can join the vote/endorsement; the purpose is shown; it is not decided by the employer's intention |
| Record of selection | Keep the vote result/endorsement process so you can explain who was chosen and for what |
| Notification | Concluded agreements and work rules must be made known to workers by delivery, posting, electronic means, etc. (Article 106) |
| No disadvantageous treatment | Do not treat someone disadvantageously for being, seeking to be, or acting properly as the representative (Enforcement Rules Article 6-2(3)) |

Article 36 notifications can also be filed electronically. Check the current form on e-Gov before filing. For systems that need the representative's agreement, such as digital payment and hourly annual leave, see [digital wage payment and the labour-management agreement](/en/labor/column/chingin-digital-barai-roushi-kyotei), [introducing hourly annual leave and the agreement/work rules](/en/labor/column/jikantan-nenkyu-dounyu-roushi-kyotei-shugyokisoku) and [MHLW guidance on planned annual leave](https://work-holiday.mhlw.go.jp/planned-granting/procedure.html).

## Frequently asked questions

**Q. Can we make the person the president says he "wants to ask" the majority representative?**
A. Not appropriate. Enforcement Rules Article 6-2(1)(ii) requires the person not to be chosen based on the employer's intention. A representative the employer nominates does not meet the requirement, and the 36 agreement that representative concludes may be invalid. You must show which agreement or filing the person is being chosen for, and have all workers choose by a democratic procedure such as a vote, show of hands or endorsement.

**Q. Can a section manager or plant manager be the majority representative?**
A. For an Article 36 agreement, a person who is a manager/supervisor under Article 41, item (ii) cannot be the majority representative (Enforcement Rules Article 6-2(1)(i)). But whether someone is a manager/supervisor is judged by substance, not title; a "manager in name only" without matching authority or treatment is not a manager/supervisor and can be the representative. The denominator for "majority" counts managers/supervisors too.

**Q. If we chose the wrong way, what happens to a 36 agreement already filed?**
A. An Article 36 agreement signed by an improperly selected representative cannot authorize work beyond statutory hours or on statutory days off, even if filed. In Tokoro (Supreme Court, Second Petty Bench, 22 June 2001), the head of an employee social club could not automatically serve as the workers’ representative. Where no majority union exists, select a representative properly, conclude a new agreement and file it before the relevant work. A new agreement cannot retroactively legalize earlier practices.

**Q. We are few in number; can we choose by email?**
A. Choosing by email, intranet or electronic voting is unobjectionable in itself, as long as all workers can join, the purpose is shown, and it is not decided by the employer's intention. Keep a record of the process so you can explain who was chosen and for what. Concluded agreements and work rules are made known by delivery, posting, electronic means, etc. (Article 106). Confirm support from a majority of all workers at the workplace, not just from those who vote. Do not count a non-response as support.
