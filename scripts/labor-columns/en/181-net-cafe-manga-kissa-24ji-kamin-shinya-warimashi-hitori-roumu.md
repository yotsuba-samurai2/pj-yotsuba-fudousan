## Is a night-shift nap working time?

Even without actual service during a nap, if staying in the nap room or on the premises is required and you must respond to customers, calls or trouble, so release from work is not guaranteed, the nap counts as working time as a whole. This is the view in the Oboshi Building Management case (Supreme Court First Petty Bench, 28 February 2002), which recognised as working time under Article 32 of the Labour Standards Act the nap time of a building-management employee on an overnight shift.

| How the nap is placed | View on working-time status |
|---|---|
| Staying on the premises/nap room required; duty to respond to customers/calls | Release from work not guaranteed = working time as a whole |
| Free to leave the worksite, no call-outs, a complete break | Release from work guaranteed = break time |
| Called a "nap" but reception or patrol in fact continues | Actual work itself = working time |

What matters is **the reality of whether release from work is guaranteed, not the name "nap" or "break"**. Leaving one person to run night reception while "napping" is not release from work, so it is treated as working time. The difference between night shift and overnight duty is in [How night shift and overnight duty differ at a group home](/en/labor/column/group-home-yakin-shukuchoku-roudou-chigai), and the security-industry view of nap time in [Is a security guard's nap working time](/en/labor/column/keibigyo-kamin-dansoku-roudoujikan-roumu).

## When and how much is the late-night premium?

For work in the band from 10 p.m. to 5 a.m., you pay a late-night premium of 25% or more of the ordinary wage (Labour Standards Act Article 37(4)). This applies to naps and standby too, so long as the time counts as working time. Overtime beyond statutory hours (8 a day / 40 a week, Article 32) and statutory-holiday work add further premiums.

| Category | Basis | Premium rate |
|---|---|---|
| Overtime | Labour Standards Act Article 37(1) | 25% or more |
| Overtime beyond 60 hours a month | Same (applied to SMEs too from 1 April 2023) | 50% or more |
| Statutory-holiday work | Article 37(1) | 35% or more |
| Late-night work (10 p.m.–5 a.m.) | Article 37(4) | 25% or more |

Where they overlap, the rates add up (e.g. late-night overtime is 25% + 25% = 50% or more). Treating a late-night nap as an "unpaid break" easily leads to unpaid-wage problems. A manager still needs the late-night premium; the relationship between a nominal title and premium wages is in [Nominal managers and the late-night premium](/en/labor/column/kanri-kantokusha-nabakari-warimashi-shinya).

## Can you give a break on a one-person shift, and how is standby time treated?

Where working time exceeds six hours you must give at least 45 minutes, and where it exceeds eight hours at least one hour, of break during the working time (Labour Standards Act Article 34(1)). A break must be freely usable (Article 34(3)); minding the store or reception is not a break. Time spent alone standing ready for customers is standby time, not guaranteed release from work, so it is working time, not a break.

| Type of time | Working time? | View |
|---|---|---|
| Break time | No | Time with a guaranteed right to be released, freely usable (Article 34) |
| Standby time | Yes | Waiting for customers or instructions, unable to leave the employer's direction |

To actually give a break on a one-person shift, you need a way to release the worker — bringing in cover for that time or switching to unmanned handling. The idea of patrol/standby at an unmanned store is set out in [Is patrol/standby time at an unmanned 24-hour store working time](/en/labor/column/mujin-24ji-tenpo-coin-laundry-junkai-machijikan-roumu). An exception to giving breaks all at once is allowed for some industries such as amusement and catering, but whether your store falls in such a category turns on the reality, so we do not conclude here (**unverified**).

## How should you design shifts for long operating hours?

A 24-hour operation has large peaks and troughs, hard to fit into a fixed 8-hour day. A variable working-hours system (one-month unit: Labour Standards Act Article 32-2; one-year unit: Article 32-4) averages hours over a period to within statutory hours, so staffing longer on busy days and shorter on slow days can avoid overtime treatment if the average stays within the limit. But the type, and how it is set in work rules and labour-management agreements, is detailed, and a design error still produces overtime premiums.

| System | Basis | Suited to |
|---|---|---|
| One-month variable working hours | Labour Standards Act Article 32-2 | Peaks by day/weekday within a month |
| One-year variable working hours | Article 32-4 | Seasonal/monthly peaks (agreement and filing needed) |
| 36 Agreement (overtime/holiday cap) | Article 36 | The premise where overtime remains |

Even with a variable system, the overtime cap (Article 36) and concluding/filing a 36 Agreement are the premise. The scope a 36 Agreement can allow is in [How far can a 36 Agreement allow overtime](/en/labor/column/36-kyotei-zangyo-sharoushi-doko-made), choosing variable hours in [Choosing variable working hours and flextime](/en/labor/column/henkei-roudoujikan-flextime-erabikata), and when work rules become mandatory in [From how many employees are work rules mandatory](/en/labor/column/shugyokisoku-10nin-gimu-nani-ga-hitsuyo).

## What should you do for health on long late-night shifts?

Duty including late-night work has health-protection measures. A worker regularly engaged in specified duty including late-night work needs a health check once every six months (Industrial Safety and Health Regulations Article 45). Where a worker in late-night work submits the result of a health check they took themselves, the employer takes measures based on it (Industrial Safety and Health Act Article 66-2). For a worker with accumulated fatigue from long hours, a doctor's interview guidance is given on the worker's request (Article 66-8).

| Measure | Basis | Content |
|---|---|---|
| Specified-duty health check | Industrial Safety and Health Regulations Article 45 | Once every six months for those regularly in late-night duty |
| Voluntary late-night health check | Industrial Safety and Health Act Article 66-2 | Submission of a self-taken result and employer response |
| Interview guidance for long hours | Article 66-8 | Doctor's interview for those with accumulated fatigue |

A continuous one-person late-night setup is also risky because there is no cover if someone is unwell. The specific frequency and target hours of checks and interviews turn on the reality of the duty, so we do not conclude on the individual case here (**unverified**). The duty design of a facility with night shifts is also useful in [Staffing and night-shift labour of a fee-charging home for the elderly](/en/labor/column/tokutei-shisetsu-yuryo-home-jinin-haichi-yakin-roumu).

## Frequently asked questions

**Q. Can a night-shift nap be an unpaid break?**
A. Not across the board. Where staying on the premises or in the nap room is required and there is a duty to respond to customers, calls or trouble, so release from work is not guaranteed, it is working time as a whole even while asleep (the general view from the Oboshi Building Management case, Supreme Court First Petty Bench, 28 February 2002). It can be an unpaid break only where it is a complete break with free exit and no call-outs. Which the reality amounts to turns on the specific arrangement.

**Q. When and how much is the late-night premium?**
A. For work in the band from 10 p.m. to 5 a.m., a late-night premium of 25% or more of the ordinary wage is required (Labour Standards Act Article 37(4)). It applies to naps and standby so long as the time counts as working time. Where it overlaps with overtime (25% or more) or statutory-holiday work (35% or more), the rates add up.

**Q. Must you give a break even on a one-person late-night shift?**
A. Yes. Where working time exceeds six hours you must give 45 minutes, and over eight hours one hour, of break during the working time (Labour Standards Act Article 34(1)). A break must be freely usable (Article 34(3)); standing by while minding the store is not a break. To give a real break on a one-person shift, you need a way to release the worker, such as bringing in cover for that time.

**Q. If you use a variable working-hours system, does overtime pay disappear?**
A. No. A variable working-hours system (Labour Standards Act Article 32-2/32-4) averages hours over a period to within statutory hours; work beyond the average needs an overtime premium. The late-night premium arises separately even under a variable system. The type and how it is set in work rules and agreements is detailed, and a design error still produces premiums.
