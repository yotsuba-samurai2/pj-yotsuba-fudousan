**Bottom line first**: For sites where shipping volume swings sharply within the month and across seasons—logistics warehouses and EC fulfillment centers—the basic design uses a monthly (Article 32-2 of the Labor Standards Act) or yearly (Article 32-4) variable working-hour system to change the day-by-day distribution without moving the total, filling the peaks and troughs with short-time part-timers. A variable system's very manner of being set is a requirement; omissions recalculate the whole period at 8 hours a day and 40 hours a week, turning the excess all into overtime. Social-insurance coverage for short-time workers turns on tests such as 20 hours a week, and the authoritative record lives in the work rules, wage rules and labor-management agreement.

## When peaks and troughs are large at a logistics warehouse, which variable working-hour system fits?

EC fulfillment centers and mail-order warehouses are sites with large swings: shipping spikes before sales, year-end and long weekends, and settles at other times. Trying to ride out the busy season under the plain principle of 8 hours a day and 40 hours a week (Article 32 of the Labor Standards Act) makes overtime chronic and swells the burden of premium pay and the Article 36 agreement. So you use a variable working-hour system that averages a set period to within 40 hours a week, assigning longer scheduled hours in busy periods and offsetting them with shorter hours in slow periods.

| System | Basis | Framework | Suited to |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monthly variable working-hour system | Article 32-2 | Average within 40 hours a week over a period of up to one month; set by work rules or a labor-management agreement | Peaks and troughs at month-end/start or in particular weeks |
| Yearly variable working-hour system | Article 32-4 | Average within 40 hours a week over the target period (over one month, up to one year); a labor-management agreement is mandatory, filed with the head of the competent Labor Standards Inspection Office | Clear seasonal swings |

Which you choose turns on the cycle of the swing. If peaks and troughs repeat within a month, the monthly system; if the swing is large in a year-end rush or a particular season, the yearly system. For choosing the system itself, see [choosing between variable working hours and flextime](/en/labor/column/henkei-roudoujikan-flextime-erabikata). The point to watch is that a variable working-hour system is a device for changing the day-by-day distribution while leaving the total untouched; it does not reduce or exempt overtime. Anything beyond the pre-set frame is treated as overtime as usual, needing an [Article 36 agreement](/en/labor/column/36-kyotei-zangyo-sharoushi-doko-made) and premium pay. For centers running shipping at night, note too that work between 10 p.m. and 5 a.m. carries the separate night premium of 25% or more under Article 37, paragraph 4, to be fixed in the wage rules.

## How do you use the yearly and monthly variable working-hour systems differently?

The yearly system has a larger frame, and in exchange its ceilings are stricter. The limit is 10 hours a day and 52 hours a week; where the target period exceeds three months the number of working days is capped at 280 a year, and the number of consecutive working days is in principle 6 (even for a special period set by agreement, within a range that secures one day of rest a week). These consecutive-day and weekly limits are set by Article 12-4 of the Ordinance for Enforcement of the Labor Standards Act. You cannot schedule unlimited consecutive work just because it is a busy season.

| Axis | Monthly | Yearly |
|---|---|---|
| Target period | Up to one month | Over one month, up to one year |
| Daily/weekly ceiling | No particular ceiling (distribute within the total) | 10 hours a day, 52 hours a week |
| Annual working days | No limit | Up to 280 a year where the target period exceeds three months |
| Consecutive working days | No limit (one rest day a week, etc.) | In principle 6 (within a one-rest-day-a-week range for a special period) |
| How it is set | Work rules or a labor-management agreement | A labor-management agreement is mandatory + filed with the Inspection Office |

In practice, you design the large year-round swing roughly with the yearly system and, where it cannot absorb the fine within-month peaks and troughs, switch to the monthly system by center. The yearly system requires fixing each working day and its hours in advance over the target period, so it is hard to move shifts freely midway; for sites where volume is hard to read, the monthly system can be easier to run. Both become valid only once the labor-management agreement, work rules and work-schedule table that set the frame are all in place.

## How do social-insurance coverage and labor-condition disclosure work for short-time part-timers?

In a design that fills the peaks with short-time part-timers, the coverage test and labor-condition disclosure are the issues. A short-time worker becomes an insured person under health insurance and employees' pension insurance where all of the following are met (Article 3, paragraph 1, item 9 of the Health Insurance Act).

| Requirement | Content |
|---|---|
| Scheduled hours | Scheduled working hours of 20 hours or more a week |
| Wage | Scheduled monthly wage of 88,000 yen or more (overtime pay, commuting and bonus allowances etc. excluded from the calculation) |
| Employment period | Expected to be used continuously for more than two months |
| Not a student | Daytime students are in principle outside |
| Scale of the workplace | Used at a specified covered workplace (the total number of employees' pension insured persons regularly exceeds 50, i.e. 51 or more) |

The workplace-scale requirement was lowered from the former "regularly over 100" to "regularly over 50" on 1 October 2024 (Japan Pension Service guidance on the expansion of coverage; accessed 2 November 2026). For a company with several warehouses, insured persons are counted by legal entity, so even a small single site may fall within scope. For how social insurance treats part-timers whose weekly scheduled hours fall short of 20, see [short-time employment under 20 hours and the three-quarters test for social insurance](/en/labor/column/tanjikan-koyo-shakaihoken-4bunno3); for the coming flow of the expansion, see [the expansion of social-insurance coverage (2027)](/en/labor/column/shakaihoken-tekiyo-kakudai-2027) and [the expansion of employment insurance to 10 hours a week (2028)](/en/labor/column/koyohoken-tekiyo-kakudai-shukan10jikan-2028).

Labor-condition disclosure is also indispensable. Article 15, paragraph 1 of the Labor Standards Act requires disclosure of labor conditions at hiring, and the amendment effective 1 April 2024 added the scope of change of workplace and duties to the disclosure items (Article 5 of the Enforcement Ordinance). For short-time and fixed-term part-timers, there is a further duty to disclose in writing the presence or absence of pay raises, retirement allowances and bonuses, and a consultation contact (Article 6 of the Act on Improvement of Employment Management etc. of Part-Time Workers and Fixed-Term Workers). It is also prohibited to set unreasonable differences in treatment against ordinary workers (Article 8, balanced treatment), and discriminatory treatment is prohibited for part-timers whose job content and scope of change are the same as ordinary workers (Article 9, equal treatment). For the whole picture of disclosure items, see [the 2024 change to the labor-condition disclosure rule](/en/labor/column/roudou-joken-meiji-2024-henko-han-i). For points on using short, one-off stints of labor only in the busy season, see [labor management for spot work and gap-time part-time jobs](/en/labor/column/spot-work-sukima-baito-roumu-kanri).

## What should you watch for in labor terms when taking on foreign workers (specified skilled worker, training employment) at a warehouse?

Foreign-worker acceptance is advancing on warehouse and logistics sites, but the basic labor rules are no different from those for Japanese workers. The Labor Standards Act, the Minimum Wage Act and social insurance apply regardless of nationality, and you cannot set differences in wages or labor conditions for the same work on the ground of nationality. Variable working hours, the night premium and the social-insurance coverage test are all done on the same basis as for Japanese workers.

On the residence-status side, the specified skilled worker status is a status of residence under the Immigration Control and Refugee Recognition Act, with acceptance requirements by field. The technical intern training system is scheduled to shift to the training-employment system. Training employment was created by Act No. 60 of 2024 (promulgated 21 June 2024) and is scheduled to take effect on 1 April 2027 (the effective date is fixed by cabinet order; confirm the latest with the Immigration Services Agency). The system's aim shifts to developing and securing human resources, framed to raise a worker to the level of specified skilled worker (i) over three years in principle. For the difference between technical intern training and training employment, see [the labor differences between technical intern training, training employment and specified skilled worker](/en/labor/column/ginou-jisshu-ikusei-shuro-roumu-chigai).

What to watch especially in labor terms is whether the scope of activity permitted by the residence status matches the duties the worker is actually given at the warehouse. Judging whether a residence status applies, and acting as an agent for the application, are in the domain of the Gyoseishoshi; a Shakai Hoken Roumushi cannot act as agent for a residence-status application. Because acceptability itself is committed to the residence-status examination, you set up on the labor side a design that hinges the start of the employment contract on obtaining the residence status, and the management of working hours, wages and social insurance after acceptance.

## Who handles the work rules and the Article 36 agreement, and the warehouse-business registration and residence procedures?

With a variable working-hour system, the way it is set is itself a requirement. Omissions void the system, so the whole period is recalculated at the plain 8 hours a day and 40 hours a week, and the excess all becomes subject to overtime premiums. At a minimum, cover the following.

| Document | What to write |
|---|---|
| Work rules | A statement that a variable working-hour system is used, the start/end times and breaks of each shift category, rest days, and part-timers' labor conditions (workplaces with 10 or more regular workers have a duty to prepare and file) |
| Labor-management agreement (mandatory for the yearly system) | The scope of covered workers, the target period and its start date, working days and the hours of each working day, the special period, and the valid term. File with the head of the competent Labor Standards Inspection Office |
| Article 36 agreement | Needed separately from the variable system; the limits on overtime and holiday work |
| Work-schedule table (shift table) | Fix each working day's hours in advance |

For the basic duty to prepare work rules, see [from how many employees are work rules mandatory](/en/labor/column/shugyokisoku-10nin-gimu-nani-ga-hitsuyo); for the limits of an Article 36 agreement, see [how far overtime can go under an Article 36 agreement](/en/labor/column/36-kyotei-zangyo-sharoushi-doko-made).

The division of roles splits by the governing law. Designing the variable working-hour system, preparing and filing the work rules, wage rules, labor-management agreement and Article 36 agreement, and the coverage test for short-time workers are handled by a Shakai Hoken Roumushi. The registration of a warehousing business under the Warehousing Business Act, and acting as an agent for foreign workers' residence-status applications (specified skilled worker, training employment), are handled by a Gyoseishoshi. ## Frequently asked questions

**Q. We want scheduled hours of 10 a day only in the EC warehouse's busy season—under a variable working-hour system, is no overtime pay needed?**
A. Within the scheduled hours lawfully set under a variable working-hour system, if you have a worker work beyond 8 hours on that day, you need not pay that portion as overtime premium. But this holds only where you have fixed each day's scheduled hours in advance in the work rules or a labor-management agreement. Anything beyond the set frame needs overtime premium and an Article 36 agreement as usual. Under the yearly system the ceiling is 10 hours a day and 52 hours a week, and you cannot set scheduled hours beyond that. A variable system does not make overtime pay unnecessary; it moves the distribution within the total.

**Q. We hired a part-timer working 25 hours a week in the busy season. Do we need to enroll them in social insurance?**
A. If the workplace is a specified covered workplace (a company whose total number of employees' pension insured persons regularly exceeds 50), then meeting all of the requirements—20 or more scheduled hours a week, a monthly wage of 88,000 yen or more, expected employment of more than two months, and not a student—makes them an insured person under health insurance and employees' pension insurance (Article 3, paragraph 1, item 9 of the Health Insurance Act). Twenty-five hours satisfies the 20-hours-or-more test, so they may fall within scope depending on the other requirements. Note that if scheduled hours are three-quarters or more of an ordinary worker's, they become insured regardless of company scale. The actual judgment varies with the wage breakdown and contract terms, so individual confirmation is needed.

**Q. Under a yearly variable working-hour system, may we have unlimited consecutive work in the year-end busy season?**
A. No. Even under a yearly variable working-hour system, the number of consecutive working days is in principle capped at 6, and even where a special period is set by agreement it is limited to a range that secures one rest day a week (Article 12-4 of the Enforcement Ordinance). There is also a limit of 10 hours a day and 52 hours a week, and where the target period exceeds three months, working days are capped at 280 a year. Beyond these frames the variable system's requirements are not met, and the excess needs overtime premiums and an Article 36 agreement.

**Q. When we take on foreign staff at the warehouse, are working hours and social insurance different from Japanese staff?**
A. No. The Labor Standards Act, the Minimum Wage Act and social insurance apply regardless of nationality, and variable working hours, premium wages and the coverage test are done on the same basis as for Japanese workers. You cannot set differences in wages or labor conditions on the ground of nationality. What differs is the residence-status side: for specified skilled worker and training employment (a new system scheduled to take effect on 1 April 2027), the scope of activity permitted by the residence status must match the duties actually given. Judging whether a residence status applies and acting as an agent for the application are in the Gyoseishoshi's domain; a Shakai Hoken Roumushi cannot act as agent for a residence-status application.

## Basis for this article

- Labor Standards Act (Act No. 49 of 1947), Article 32 — statutory working hours of 40 hours a week and 8 hours a day
- Article 32-2 — the monthly variable working-hour system (set by work rules or a labor-management agreement; averaging within 40 hours a week over a period of up to one month)
- Article 32-4 — the yearly variable working-hour system (by a labor-management agreement; averaging within 40 hours a week over the target period of over one month up to one year; setting the scope of workers, the target period, the special period, the working days and each day's hours and the valid term, filed with the head of the competent Labor Standards Inspection Office)
- Ordinance for Enforcement of the Labor Standards Act, Article 12-4 — for the yearly system, the 10-hour-a-day and 52-hour-a-week limits, the 280-day-a-year working-day cap where the target period exceeds three months, and the consecutive-working-day limit (in principle 6; for a special period, within a range that secures one rest day a week)
- Labor Standards Act, Article 36 — overtime and holiday work require the conclusion and filing of a labor-management agreement (Article 36 agreement); Article 37, paragraph 4 — a night premium of 25% or more for work between 10 p.m. and 5 a.m.
- Labor Standards Act, Article 15, paragraph 1 — disclosure of labor conditions at the conclusion of the labor contract; the amendment effective 1 April 2024 added the scope of change of workplace and duties to the disclosure items (Article 5 of the Enforcement Ordinance)
- Act on Improvement of Employment Management etc. of Part-Time Workers and Fixed-Term Workers (Act No. 76 of 1993), Article 6 — disclosure in writing of the presence/absence of pay raises, retirement allowances and bonuses and a consultation contact; Article 8 — prohibition of unreasonable treatment (balanced treatment); Article 9 — prohibition of discriminatory treatment against part-time and fixed-term workers to be regarded as the same as ordinary workers (equal treatment)
- Health Insurance Act (Act No. 70 of 1922), Article 3, paragraph 1, item 9 — the requirements under which a short-time worker becomes an insured person (used at a specified covered workplace, 20 or more scheduled hours a week, a monthly wage of 88,000 yen or more, expected use of more than two months, and not a student). The Employees' Pension Insurance Act has a coverage test to the same effect
- The workplace-scale requirement for a specified covered workplace was lowered to "the total number of employees' pension insured persons regularly exceeds 50" from 1 October 2024 (Japan Pension Service guidance on the expansion of social-insurance coverage; accessed 2 November 2026)
- Training-employment system — created by Act No. 60 of 2024 (amending the Immigration Control and Refugee Recognition Act etc.; promulgated 21 June 2024), scheduled to take effect on 1 April 2027, with the technical intern training system scheduled to be abolished on that date (the effective date is fixed by cabinet order; confirm the latest with the Immigration Services Agency)
- Warehousing Business Act (Act No. 121 of 1956), Article 3 — operating a warehousing business requires registration with the Minister of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism
- Statutory provisions checked on e-Gov Law Search on 2 November 2026.
