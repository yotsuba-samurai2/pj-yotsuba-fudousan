**In short:** At a funeral business, on-call standby and naps are **working time** if the worker is under the employer's direction and supervision with a duty to respond or turn out, even without actual work (Supreme Court, 9 March 2000, Mitsubishi Heavy Industries Nagasaki Shipyard; Supreme Court, 28 February 2002, Ohsei Building Management). "Night duty" that is taken out of the working-hours rules is allowed only where you obtain the **permission of the director of the labour standards inspection office** for intermittent work with ordinarily almost no need to work (Labor Standards Act, Article 41, item 3; Enforcement Ordinance, Article 23; Notice Hatsuki No. 17 of 13 September 1947). Busy-season swings are best levelled with a one-month variable working-hours system (Article 32-2); the deemed-working-hours system (Article 38-2) is generally hard to use where there is standby for immediate response. This article sets out the working-time and wage design when opening and running a funeral business, from a labour viewpoint.

"We never know when a call will come in, 24 hours a day," and "staff are called even during naps — do we have to pay for that?" — these are common questions from owners who are starting or running a funeral business. This page addresses the industry's own pattern of immediate response, on-call standby, late-night turnouts and seasonal swings, and sets out whether standby counts as working time, how night-duty permission works, and how to design a variable working-hours system and premium wages. Working-time and wage design is the work of a Shakai Hoken Roumushi; the permits for hearse transport and for cremation and cemetery matters, and the arrangement of a ceremony hall, are handled by different parties, which we set out at the end.

## Do a funeral business's on-call standby and naps have to be treated as working time?

The first point to grasp is that time spent merely "waiting at the facility or at home" can still be working time. Whether time is working time is decided not by the work rules or the label but by the objective standard of **whether the worker is under the employer's direction and supervision** (Supreme Court, 9 March 2000, Mitsubishi Heavy Industries Nagasaki Shipyard).

| Form of standby | Likely working time? | Thinking |
|---|---|---|
| Napping at the office and turning out immediately on a transport request | Likely | Not guaranteed release from work; with a duty to respond it is hand-waiting time and so working time (Supreme Court, 28 February 2002, Ohsei Building Management) |
| Waiting at the office while answering the phone | Likely | Hand-waiting time with a duty to respond; working time even without actual work |
| On-call at home (turn out when called) | Depends | Judged by how much freedom of action is restricted; some designs count only turnout time, but it depends on the reality of the constraint |
| A fully free break with no call-out and no duty to respond | Unlikely | If released from work, not working time |

In the Ohsei Building Management case, the Supreme Court held that because the building manager had a duty to respond immediately to alarms and calls and was required to stay in the nap room, the nap time was working time even without actual work. The same thinking applies to a funeral business's naps and standby if immediate response to transport or enquiries is required. The line between night shift and night duty is the same issue at a disability group home; see [night shift and night duty at a group home differ completely for labour management](/en/labor/column/group-home-yakin-shukuchoku-roudou-chigai).

## When can you obtain night-duty permission from the labour standards inspection office?

Where you want to take standby or naps out of the working-hours rules, the tool is "night/day duty." Under Article 41, item 3 of the Labor Standards Act (exemption for persons engaged in surveillance or intermittent work) and Article 23 of the Enforcement Ordinance, only where you obtain the **permission of the director of the labour standards inspection office** (Form No. 10) for intermittent work can you be exempt from the working-hours rules of Article 32. The general standard is set out in Notice Hatsuki No. 17 of 13 September 1947, roughly on the following four points.

| Item | General permission standard (Notice Hatsuki No. 17) |
|---|---|
| Content of work | A duty with ordinarily almost no need to work; the ordinary duties are not performed, limited to scheduled rounds, receiving urgent calls and documents, standing by for emergencies, and the like |
| Allowance | The allowance per night/day duty must not be less than one-third of the average daily wage per person paid to workers of the same kind |
| Frequency | In principle, night duty is limited to once a week and day duty to once a month |
| Sleep | Adequate sleeping facilities must be provided for night duty |

Here is the industry's own difficulty. Even if it is called night duty, if the reality is that **ordinary duties such as transport turnouts and dealing with the bereaved are performed frequently at night**, it does not meet "ordinarily almost no need to work" and does not qualify. That time is working time and subject to late-night and overtime premiums. The concrete line in medical and care settings is set out in [when night-duty permission can be obtained in medical and care settings](/en/labor/column/iryo-kaigo-shukujitchoku-kyoka-kijun). Whether permission is granted is decided case by case by the labour standards inspection office on the basis of the reality.

## Can a variable working-hours system or deemed hours be used for work with large busy-season swings?

A funeral business has large swings between times when ceremonies concentrate and quieter times. To reflect this in working hours, a variable working-hours system is the basic option.

| System | Basis | Suited to | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| One-month variable working-hours system | Labor Standards Act, Article 32-2 | Swings within a month; set by labour-management agreement or work rules, specifying each day's and week's scheduled hours in advance | Average 40 hours a week or less; if the advance specification breaks down, it reverts to ordinary overtime calculation |
| One-year variable working-hours system | Labor Standards Act, Article 32-4 | Seasonal swings; a labour-management agreement must be concluded and filed | Limits on working days, consecutive working days, and daily/weekly maximums |
| Deemed-working-hours system (work outside the workplace) | Labor Standards Act, Article 38-2 | Field work where working hours are hard to calculate | At a funeral business where instructions can be given at any time by mobile phone and standby can be managed, it is hard to say hours are "difficult to calculate," so generally hard to use |

In practice it is hard to use the deemed-working-hours system widely at a funeral business that carries immediate-response standby; the sound approach is to design scheduled hours with a variable working-hours system first and manage the excess as overtime. For using a variable working-hours system versus flextime, see [how to choose between a variable working-hours system and flextime](/en/labor/column/henkei-roudoujikan-flextime-erabikata). Hearse drivers may separately be subject to the Improvement Standards Notice on working hours for automobile drivers; see also [the logistics 2024 problem and the Improvement Standards Notice](/en/labor/column/unso-2024-mondai-kaizen-kijun-kokuji).

## How do you design premium wages and hand-waiting time where there are many late-night and holiday turnouts?

At a funeral business with many late-night and holiday turnouts, the design of premium wages drives both wage cost and unpaid-wage risk. The premium rates under Article 37 of the Labor Standards Act are as follows.

| Category | Premium rate | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Overtime (beyond statutory hours) | 25% or more | Labor Standards Act, Article 37, paragraph 1 |
| Portion of overtime exceeding 60 hours a month | 50% or more (applied to small and medium enterprises too, from 1 April 2023) | Article 37, paragraph 1, proviso |
| Late-night work (10 p.m.–5 a.m.) | 25% or more | Article 37, paragraph 4 |
| Statutory holiday work | 35% or more | Article 37, paragraph 1 |

As a premise for premiums, a **36 Agreement must be concluded and filed** to have workers do overtime beyond statutory hours or holiday work. Further, where the on-call standby or a nap with a duty to respond counts as working time (hand-waiting time), that time too is included in the overtime and late-night calculation. A design that "pays only for the actual turnout work" leaves pay owing once the standby itself is judged to be working time. The extinctive prescription of a wage claim is, for the time being, three years (Labor Standards Act, Article 115 and Supplementary Provisions), so three years' worth may be claimed together. For the requirements when using fixed overtime pay, see [the requirements for lawful fixed overtime pay and settlement of the difference](/en/labor/column/kotei-zangyodai-tekiho-youken-sagaku-seisan); for the pitfall of a manager in name only, see [the scope of a manager and the manager-in-name-only problem](/en/labor/column/kanri-kantokusha-nabakari-warimashi-shinya). Recording start and end times and the reality of standby objectively is the starting point of the design.

## Frequently asked questions

**Q. Do we not have to pay for time staff nap at the office ready for a transport call?**
A. You often do. If there is a duty to respond and turn out immediately and release from work is not guaranteed, it is hand-waiting time and so working time even without actual work (Supreme Court, 28 February 2002, Ohsei Building Management). If it is working time, the portion falling in late-night or overtime hours is subject to premium wages.

**Q. If we label it "night duty," can we take night-time standby out of working time?**
A. It is judged by the reality, not the label. To take night duty out of the working-hours rules, it must meet requirements such as being intermittent work with ordinarily almost no need to work, and you must obtain the permission of the director of the labour standards inspection office (Form No. 10) (Labor Standards Act, Article 41, item 3; Enforcement Ordinance, Article 23; Notice Hatsuki No. 17). Where transport and dealing with the bereaved occur frequently at night, permission is generally not granted.

**Q. Which system can we use to adjust working hours between busy and quiet seasons?**
A. A one-month (Labor Standards Act, Article 32-2) or one-year (Article 32-4) variable working-hours system is the basis. You specify each day's and week's scheduled hours in advance and keep the average within 40 hours a week. At a funeral business where instructions can be given at any time and standby can be managed, the deemed-working-hours system (Article 38-2) is hard to say is "difficult to calculate" and is generally hard to use.

**Q. Where there are many late-night turnouts, how do the premiums stack?**
A. Overtime beyond statutory hours is 25% or more; of that, the portion over 60 hours a month is 50% or more (applied to small and medium enterprises too, from 1 April 2023); late-night from 10 p.m. to 5 a.m. is 25% or more; a statutory holiday is 35% or more (Labor Standards Act, Article 37). Time that falls into more than one category, such as overtime at late night, adds the respective premium rates together.
