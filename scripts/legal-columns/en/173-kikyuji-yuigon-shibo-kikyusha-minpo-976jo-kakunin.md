**In short:** when you are so near death during hospitalisation that neither a notarised nor a holographic will can be made in time, there is the special-form option of a **will of a person in imminent danger of death**. Where a **person in imminent danger of death** by illness or another cause intends to make a will, it can be done with **three or more witnesses** in attendance by **dictating** the gist of the will to one of them; the witness who receives the dictation writes it down, reads it back to (or lets it be read by) the testator and the other witnesses, and after each witness approves the accuracy of the writing, each signs and seals it (Civil Code Article 976(1)). This will has no effect unless, **within 20 days of the day it is made**, a witness or an interested party requests confirmation from the **family court** (Article 976(4)). The family court may not confirm unless it is satisfied the will came from the testator's true intent (Article 976(5)). Further, once the testator can make a will in an ordinary form and then survives six months, this special-form will loses effect (Civil Code Article 983). This article is general information; the final judgement on whether a particular will is valid rests with a qualified professional and the family court's confirmation. ## What is a will of a person in imminent danger of death, and how does it differ from a notarised will?

Wills come in an "ordinary form" usable at any time and a "special form" usable only in special circumstances. The ordinary forms are the holographic will (Civil Code Article 968) and the notarised will (Article 969), among others; one special form is the **will of a person in imminent danger of death** (Article 976).

A notarised will is made with a notary's involvement, the original is kept at the notary's office, and it is hard to void for formal defects. But where there is no time to wait for a notary's visit during hospitalisation, or where you want to leave a will quickly while consciousness and strength remain, it may not be in time. The will of a person in imminent danger of death differs in that, in such imminent circumstances, it can be made with just three witnesses in attendance, without waiting for a notary.

| Form | Basis | Participants | When usable |
|---|---|---|---|
| Holographic will | Civil Code Art. 968 | Testator writes the whole in own hand | Normal times; you can write it yourself |
| Notarised will | Civil Code Art. 969 | Notary plus two witnesses | Normal times; high certainty |
| Will of a person in imminent danger of death | Civil Code Art. 976 | Three or more witnesses | Imminent danger of death where an ordinary form is not in time |

That said, in exchange for being easy to make, it needs the family court's confirmation within 20 days, and lapses six months after an ordinary form becomes possible. It is an emergency measure for imminent circumstances, not a form for normal times. For the normal-time holographic and notarised forms, see [Holographic versus notarised wills](https://luck428.com/legal/column/jihitsu-kosei-yuigon).

## In what situations can it be used (a person in imminent danger of death, or unable to write)?

It can be used where "a person in imminent danger of death by illness or another cause" intends to make a will (Civil Code Article 976(1)). It assumes a situation where serious illness or major injury makes death imminent and there is no room to follow an ordinary form. The existence of imminent danger is the premise for using this form.

In this form the testator need not write the characters themselves. The testator orally conveys the gist of the will to one of the witnesses (dictation), and the witness writes it down. So it may be available to someone whose hands cannot move to write a holographic will, or who cannot wait for a notary to arrive. A person who cannot speak may substitute for dictation by stating the gist through an interpreter before the witnesses (Article 976(2)); and where the testator or another witness cannot hear, the written content may be conveyed through an interpreter in place of reading it back (Article 976(3)).

| Premise | Content |
|---|---|
| Situation requirement | Imminent danger of death by illness or another cause |
| Need to write by hand | Not needed (a witness writes down the dictation) |
| If unable to speak | Substitute dictation with a statement via an interpreter (para. 2) |
| If unable to hear | Substitute reading-back via an interpreter (para. 3) |

Whether one is "in imminent danger of death" is judged individually from the medical state and objective circumstances, and is ultimately evaluated in the family court's confirmation or in litigation. The degree of imminence is not something this article can state conclusively.

## With the requirements of three witnesses, dictation, writing and reading-back, where does a slip void it?

A will of a person in imminent danger of death can be voided by missing even one requirement. The steps are: (1) three or more witnesses attend; (2) the testator dictates the gist of the will to one of them; (3) the witness who received the dictation writes it down; (4) the writing is read back to (or read by) the testator and the other witnesses; and (5) after each witness approves the accuracy of the writing, each signs and seals it (Civil Code Article 976(1)).

Especially easy to slip on are witness eligibility (disqualifications) and the substance of "dictation." Witnesses have disqualifications: minors, presumed heirs and devisees and their spouses and lineal relatives, and the notary's spouse and relatives within the fourth degree cannot be witnesses (Civil Code Article 974). Putting an interested relative in as a witness risks leaving too few witnesses and voiding the will. And "dictation" means the testator conveying the content of the will to the witnesses of their own intent; a form where the testator merely nods to text drawn up by others risks not being recognised as dictation.

| Requirement | What happens if missed |
|---|---|
| Three or more witnesses | If even one disqualified person is mixed in, leaving effectively two, risk of voidness |
| Dictation | If the testator does not state the gist themselves, risk of not being recognised as dictation |
| Writing and reading-back | Lacking the reading-back (viewing) and approval of accuracy risks voidness |
| Signing and sealing | Lacking each witness's signature and seal risks voidness |

Because many requirements must be met in a short time amid imminence, the selection of witnesses and the design of the procedure matter. Whether the requirements are met is ultimately evaluated in the family court's confirmation discussed below, or in litigation if disputed.

## What does the family court's confirmation within 20 days do, and who requests it?

A will of a person in imminent danger of death is not finalised in effect merely by being made. Unless, **within 20 days of the day the will is made**, a witness or an interested party requests the family court's **confirmation**, the will has no effect (Civil Code Article 976(4)). The family court may not confirm unless it is satisfied the will came from the testator's true intent (Article 976(5)). Confirmation is thus a procedure by which the family court examines whether the will rests on the testator's genuine intent.

Here a demarcation point requires care. Requesting the family court's confirmation is an application to a court (an adjudication procedure), and **preparing or acting on documents submitted to a court is not within an administrative scrivener's scope**. What the Administrative Scriveners Act makes an administrative scrivener's work is preparing documents submitted to public offices; documents submitted to a court are not included (Administrative Scriveners Act, Articles 1-2 and 1-3). This part is done by the person themselves or entrusted to a lawyer.

| Procedure | Content | Who does it |
|---|---|---|
| Request for confirmation | Request to the family court within 20 days of the will's date | A witness or an interested party (the person, a lawyer) |
| The court's confirmation | Examines whether it came from the testator's true intent | The family court |
| Effect of confirmation | Effect arises only upon confirmation (it does not finalise validity) | — |

Confirmation looks at "whether it is the testator's true intent"; it does not ultimately guarantee the will's content is valid. The will's validity may still be disputed among the heirs after confirmation. As the 20-day period is short, preparation to proceed immediately after making the will is needed. For revocation and conflict of wills, see [Revocation and conflict of wills](https://luck428.com/legal/column/yuigon-tekkai-teishoku-fukusu-yuigon-yuretsu).

## Once an ordinary form becomes possible, what happens to this will (lapse in six months)?

Because a will of a person in imminent danger of death is an emergency measure, it finishes its role once the imminent situation passes and an ordinary form becomes possible. The Civil Code provides that a will made in a special form has no effect where the testator **survives six months** from the time they become able to make a will in an ordinary form (Civil Code Article 983). Where health recovers, a holographic or notarised will becomes possible, and six months pass in that state, the earlier will of a person in imminent danger of death lapses.

| Situation | Fate of the will of a person in imminent danger of death |
|---|---|
| Died while still in imminence (confirmed) | Treated as a confirmed will |
| Recovered to a state where an ordinary form is possible | Lapses on surviving six months from then (Article 983) |
| Made a notarised will, etc., anew after recovery | The new will governs (where the content conflicts, the later will prevails) |

So, if recovery occurs, it is safer to remake the will in a certain form such as a notarised will. Understanding the will of a person in imminent danger of death as a "bridge" until a notarised will is made makes its positioning harder to mistake. The judgement on lapse and remaking is made in consultation with a qualified professional according to the situation.

## FAQ

**Q. How many witnesses are needed to make a will of a person in imminent danger of death?**
A. Three or more witnesses must attend (Civil Code Article 976(1)). Moreover, witnesses have disqualifications: minors, presumed heirs and devisees and their spouses and lineal relatives, and the notary's spouse and relatives within the fourth degree cannot be witnesses (Article 974). Putting an interested relative in as a witness risks leaving too few valid witnesses and voiding the will. Even amid imminence, securing three non-disqualified witnesses is the premise.

**Q. After making the will, is the family-court procedure always required?**
A. Yes. A will of a person in imminent danger of death has no effect unless, within 20 days of the day it is made, a witness or an interested party requests the family court's confirmation (Civil Code Article 976(4)). Because this request for confirmation is a procedure before a court, it is done by the person themselves or a lawyer and is not within an administrative scrivener's scope (Administrative Scriveners Act, Articles 1-2 and 1-3). As the 20-day period is short, prepare to proceed immediately after making the will.

**Q. What happens to this will if health recovers?**
A. If the testator survives six months from the time they become able to make a will in an ordinary form (holographic, notarised, etc.), the will of a person in imminent danger of death loses effect (Civil Code Article 983), because it is an emergency measure for imminent circumstances. Where recovery occurs, it is safer to remake the will in a certain form such as a notarised will.

**Q. Does the family court's confirmation finalise the will's content?**
A. Confirmation is a procedure by which the family court examines whether the will came from the testator's true intent; it does not ultimately guarantee that the content is valid (Civil Code Article 976(5)). The will's validity may still be disputed among the heirs after confirmation. Handling a dispute over validity is a field for a lawyer.
