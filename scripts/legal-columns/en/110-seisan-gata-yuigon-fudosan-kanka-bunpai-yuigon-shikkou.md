**In short:** a liquidation-type will has the executor sell real estate and other assets and divide the remaining proceeds among heirs or beneficiaries. It suits cases where the property is hard to divide in kind. Registration for the sale is handled by a judicial scrivener and the tax on the proceeds by a tax accountant, engaged separately.

**Information checked: 11 October 2026.** This article organises the design of a liquidation-type will (seisan-gata izō), which sells property and divides the money, and the flow of execution by the executor. The basis is the Civil Code provisions on the executor, with the articles checked on e-Gov.

## What is a liquidation-type will? (its relation to division by conversion)

A liquidation-type will directs that property (typically real estate) be sold and converted to cash, and the proceeds distributed to heirs or beneficiaries. As opposed to division in kind, where the property is divided as it stands, dividing the proceeds after a sale is called division by conversion. A liquidation-type will sets out this division by conversion in the will and entrusts the sale and distribution to the executor.

The executor's role is set by the Civil Code. Civil Code Article 1012(1) provides that "the executor has the rights and duties to perform any and all acts necessary to manage the inherited property and otherwise execute the will, in order to realise the contents of the will." Civil Code Article 1015 provides that an act done by the executor within authority, shown to be as executor, takes effect directly against the heirs. These amended provisions took effect on 1 July 2019.

## When is selling and dividing better than dividing the property in kind?

| Suitable situation | Reason |
| --- | --- |
| Several heirs who want a fair cash split | Avoids co-ownership shares; divides by amount |
| Heirs are distant or busy and co-ownership is hard to manage | A sale and settlement leaves no management behind |
| There are no heirs and a gift to a third party is intended | The proceeds can go to a specified person or body |

If co-ownership is left in place, future sale and management require everyone's agreement, and a split of views stalls it. Selling and dividing the proceeds lets the basis of distribution be set in the will. However, a sale takes time and cost, and there is no guarantee of selling at the market price. Where an heir wants to keep the property in kind, division by conversion is not always best.

## What does the executor do in the sale and distribution? (order of registration and settlement)

A typical flow is as follows. Under Civil Code Article 1012(2) only the executor may perform a bequest, and Civil Code Article 1013(1) prohibits an heir, where there is an executor, from a disposition that obstructs the execution of the will.

| Order | Main work | Who |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Taking up the executor role; confirming heirs and property | Executor |
| 2 | Registration for the sale (inheritance registration, then transfer to the buyer) | Judicial scrivener |
| 3 | Sale of the property (finding a buyer, contract, delivery) | Real-estate company |
| 4 | Settling costs and tax from the proceeds and distributing the rest | Executor |

For a liquidation-type bequest, the change of title to the real estate commonly proceeds through an inheritance registration to the heirs' names first, then a transfer of ownership to the buyer. The order of registration and the treatment of the applicant differ by case, and registration is handled by a judicial scrivener. The executor advances the sale and distribution and reports the settlement to the heirs and beneficiaries.

## Who bears the tax on the sale proceeds (quasi-final return)?

Note that the quasi-final return and the return on the sale proceeds are different. The quasi-final return covers the decedent's income from 1 January to the date of death, filed by the heirs within four months from the day after they learn of the commencement of inheritance. Because a sale under a liquidation-type will occurs after inheritance commences, its proceeds (capital gains) are in principle not covered by the quasi-final return; they are attributed to the heirs (or beneficiaries) who are the sellers, and each files their own return.

If inherited real estate is sold within a set period, a special rule may allow part of the inheritance-tax amount to be added to the acquisition cost ("acquisition-cost addition"). Who is taxed and how much, and whether the rule applies, depend on how the property was acquired and the holding period. Tax calculation and filing are the tax accountant's role. This article does not give individual tax amounts.

## How should the will be drafted to avoid trouble?

- State clearly the purpose of selling and dividing the proceeds (division by conversion) and the shares and order of distribution
- Appoint an executor and set out specific authority to sell, apply for registration, terminate contracts and settle (also keep in mind the difference from a specified-property succession will under Civil Code Article 1014)
- Set a policy on the minimum sale price and timing, and the treatment if it does not sell
- Write the order in which costs (registration, brokerage, tax) are deducted from the proceeds

Whether to make it a notarial deed is discussed with a notary. Infringing a reserved portion (the minimum share held by heirs other than siblings) can lead to later disputes, so design the distribution carefully. Where a dispute is anticipated, or for judgements on the reserved portion or the will's validity, consult a lawyer.

## FAQ

**Q. How does a liquidation-type will differ from division by conversion?**
A. Division by conversion is the name of a method of dividing an estate by selling it and splitting the proceeds; a liquidation-type will sets this out in the will and entrusts the sale and distribution to the executor. Division by conversion is also possible by a division agreement.

**Q. Can one of the heirs serve as the executor?**
A. Yes. But the sale, registration, settlement and distribution are a heavy burden, and interests among heirs can conflict. Considering neutrality and effort, appointing a professional is also an option. Registration is handled by a judicial scrivener.

**Q. Does the executor pay the tax on the sale proceeds?**
A. No. Proceeds (capital gains) arising after inheritance commences are in principle attributed to the heirs or beneficiaries who are the sellers, and each files their own return. The quasi-final return concerns the decedent's lifetime income. Confirm the calculation with a tax accountant.

**Q. Is a liquidation-type will that infringes the reserved portion void?**
A. It is not immediately void, but an heir whose reserved portion is infringed may make a monetary claim. For the design of the distribution and the response where a dispute is anticipated, consult a lawyer.
