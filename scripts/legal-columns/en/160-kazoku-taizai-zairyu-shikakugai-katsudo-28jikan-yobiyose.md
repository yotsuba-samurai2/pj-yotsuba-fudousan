**In short:** the Dependent status of residence is for a spouse or child supported by a person who resides in Japan on a work or similar status, to carry on daily activities (Immigration Control and Refugee Recognition Act, Appended Table I(4)). To bring them over, the premise is that the supporting person (the supporter) has the means to support them and holds a work or study status of residence. A Dependent holder cannot, in principle, work; but with permission to engage in an activity other than that permitted (Immigration Control Act Article 19(2); the permitting authority is the Commissioner of the Immigration Services Agency), within the comprehensive permission they may work up to 28 hours a week. However, work at a workplace where an adult-entertainment or sex-related business operates is excluded. If they work beyond 28 hours, the person may become subject to revocation of status or deportation, and the employer may be charged with the offence of facilitating unlawful work (Immigration Control Act Article 73-2). The penalty for this offence was raised by the 2024 amendment (Act No. 60 of 2024). ## Who, and what kind of family, can be brought over on a Dependent status?

The Dependent status of residence is for a "spouse" or "child" supported by a foreign national residing in Japan on a work or similar status, to lead a daily life in Japan. The basis is Appended Table I(4) of the Immigration Control and Refugee Recognition Act, which sets out the "Dependent" status and the corresponding activity (daily activities carried on as a spouse or child supported by a person residing under a certain status of residence). Those who can be brought over are, in principle, a spouse and children; the person's parents or siblings do not qualify for Dependent status.

Here, "supported by" means a relationship of economic dependence on the supporter. For a spouse, there must be the reality of living together and being supported; a marriage in name only does not qualify. For a child, it is judged by the reality of support whether the child is a minor or an adult; but where the child has reached adulthood and makes an independent living, it may be judged not to fit Dependent status. The framework of this Appended Table is the same when bringing a spouse or child over from mainland China, Taiwan and so on. The "Spouse or Child of Japanese National" status, used to bring over a Japanese person's spouse, is a different status of residence with different requirements ([Renewal and change of the Spouse of Japanese National visa](https://luck428.com/legal/column/nihonjin-haiguusha-biza-zairyu-koshin-henko-chuka-taiwan)).

## Does the person bringing them over (the supporter) have income or status conditions?

Yes. Because Dependent status is the status of a family member "being supported," the supporter's status of residence and means are at the centre of the examination. The supporter must hold a status of residence that can be the basis of a Dependent status — a work status (Engineer/Specialist in Humanities/International Services, Highly Skilled Professional, Specified Skilled Worker, etc.) or study. On top of that, whether they have the means — income and savings — to support the family is examined.

| Item | Yardstick |
|---|---|
| Family who can be brought over | A supported spouse and children (parents and siblings excluded) |
| Supporter's status of residence | A work status (Engineer/Specialist in Humanities/International Services, Highly Skilled Professional, Specified Skilled Worker, etc.) or study — a status that can be the basis of a Dependent status |
| Supporter's means | Income and savings sufficient to support the spouse and children |
| Work on Dependent status | Not allowed in principle; up to 28 hours a week is possible with activity-outside-status permission |

Where the supporter is a student, whether the family can be supported on the person's part-time income alone is examined strictly. How to make out the reality of support and the means is built up with materials such as income certificates, certificates of employment and remittance records. Because how far which materials suffice depends on the individual circumstances, it is important to arrange them before applying. The basic requirements of other work statuses are covered in [Point calculation for Highly Skilled Professional (i)](https://luck428.com/legal/column/kodo-senmonshoku-1go-point-keisan-chuka-taiwan), and the move from Specified Skilled Worker in [Requirements for changing to Specified Skilled Worker (i) and (ii)](https://luck428.com/legal/column/tokutei-ginou-1go-2go-ikou-zairyu-henko-youken).

## Can a Dependent holder work, and what is the 28-hour activity-outside-status?

Because Dependent status is a status for "being supported," in principle they cannot engage in work with income. To work, they must obtain permission to engage in an activity other than that permitted. This permission is granted under Immigration Control Act Article 19(2) by the Commissioner of the Immigration Services Agency, when the Commissioner finds it reasonable within a range that does not hinder the carrying out of the activity corresponding to the status of residence. What Dependent and study holders can generally obtain is "comprehensive permission," which allows activity with income of up to 28 hours a week.

That said, not any job will do. Even with comprehensive permission, work at a workplace where an adult-entertainment or sex-related business operates is not allowed. Also, 28 hours is an upper limit "per week," and where several part-time jobs are held, it is judged on the total. Obtaining the permission before starting work, and not exceeding the scope of the permission (hours and type of business), matters for both the person and the employer. Note that the operation of activity-outside-status is subject to review, so check the latest treatment in the Immigration Services Agency's guidance.

## If they work beyond 28 hours, what happens to the person and the employer?

Exceeding the limit carries risk for both the person and the employer. On the person's side, work beyond the scope of the permission becomes an activity in violation of Immigration Control Act Article 19(1), and can lead to revocation of the status of residence, deportation, and subsequent refusal of renewal or change. Habitually exceeding on the thought that "just a little is fine" endangers the person's own residence.

On the employer's side, they may be charged, as a person who had someone engage in unlawful work, with the offence of facilitating unlawful work (Immigration Control Act Article 73-2). What matters is that Article 73-2(2) provides that, even where the employer did not know the other party was engaged in unlawful work, they cannot escape punishment unless there was no negligence. In other words, if they did not check — through the residence card and the like — whether there was activity-outside-status permission and its hour limit, "I didn't know" will not readily excuse them. The penalty for this offence was raised by the 2024 amendment (Act No. 60 of 2024) from the former "imprisonment for up to 3 years or a fine of up to 3 million yen" to "imprisonment for up to 5 years or a fine of up to 5 million yen (these may also be imposed together)" (confirm the details of the enforcement date in the official gazette and the Ministry of Justice's notice). A corporation may also be fined under the dual-liability provision. Checking the residence card and activity-outside-status permission at hiring and during employment is itself the employer's risk management.

## When a child advances in school or reaches adulthood, how is the status of residence switched?

It is possible to advance to a Japanese high school or university while on Dependent status. What tends to become an issue is when the child grows up and takes a job, or reaches adulthood and becomes independent. Because Dependent status is premised on "being supported," to take a full-time job in Japan the child needs a work status matching the content of the work (Engineer/Specialist in Humanities/International Services, etc.), or a change of status to "Designated Activities," which is recognised under certain requirements. Once they leave support and become independent, remaining on Dependent status is not originally envisaged.

That said, which status can be changed to is a part with high individuality, where the conclusion divides according to circumstances such as the child's academic record, the content of the work and the time of arrival in Japan. For a child who came to Japan young and grew up here, there is a treatment under which, under certain requirements, a change to a status with no work restriction is recognised; but whether it applies is a judgement case by case. We recommend charting the prospects of the status of residence early, once the course is decided. The final judgement on which status applies and whether a change is recognised is made by a qualified professional and the Immigration Services Agency.

## FAQ

**Q. Can I bring my parents or siblings over on a Dependent status?**
A. No. Those who can be brought over on a Dependent status are, in principle, limited to a supported spouse and children (Immigration Control Act, Appended Table I(4)). Parents and siblings do not qualify for Dependent status. Bringing over an elderly parent and the like means examining, case by case, whether another status of residence (such as Designated Activities) is possible.

**Q. Can a Dependent family member work without permission?**
A. No. A Dependent holder cannot, in principle, work; to work they need permission to engage in an activity other than that permitted (Immigration Control Act Article 19(2)). With comprehensive permission they may work up to 28 hours a week, but work at a workplace where an adult-entertainment or sex-related business operates is not allowed.

**Q. What happens if they work a little beyond 28 hours?**
A. The person is in violation beyond the scope of the permission, which can lead to revocation of status, deportation, and refusal of renewal or change. The employer may be charged with the offence of facilitating unlawful work (Immigration Control Act Article 73-2) and cannot escape punishment even if they did not know the other party was working unlawfully, unless there was no negligence (Article 73-2(2)).

**Q. What is needed for a Dependent child to take a job in Japan?**
A. Taking a full-time job while on Dependent status is in principle not possible; a work status matching the content of the work (Engineer/Specialist in Humanities/International Services, etc.), or a change to "Designated Activities" recognised under certain requirements, is needed. Which status applies is an individual judgement based on circumstances such as academic record, the content of the work and the time of arrival in Japan.
