**In short:** a liquidation-type bequest (seisangata izo) sells assets such as real estate (conversion to money) and leaves the proceeds to heirs or beneficiaries. The executor handles the sale and distribution, and in practice registration is two steps: an inheritance registration first, then transfer to the buyer. Because the attribution of the capital gain can be viewed differently depending on the case, confirm the wording with an administrative scrivener, the registration with a judicial scrivener, and the tax with a tax accountant.

Updated: 2026-10-10

## How does a liquidation-type bequest differ from a "give-by-inheritance" will?

A "give-by-inheritance" will passes a specific asset in kind to a specific heir; in the Civil Code this is called a specified-property succession will. A liquidation-type bequest, by contrast, does not pass the asset in kind: the executor first sells it (converts it to money) and distributes the proceeds. It is used where real estate is hard to divide, where heirs do not want the asset in kind, or where the estate's debts are to be settled from the sale.

| Comparison | Specified-property succession will | Liquidation-type bequest |
|---|---|---|
| How it passes | Asset passes in kind | Sold and proceeds distributed |
| Execution | Passed by registration | Executor sells and distributes proceeds |
| Basis of execution | Civil Code Article 1014 | Civil Code Article 1012 (executor's powers) |
| Suited to | Leaving the asset itself | Leaving hard-to-divide assets as cash |

Which fits depends on the asset mix and the heirs' wishes. An administrative scrivener supports drafting the will; a final judgement on legal effect, or an assessment of likely disputes, is the domain of an attorney.

## In whose name, and how, does the executor sell the real estate?

Under Civil Code Article 1012(1), the executor has the right and duty to do everything necessary to carry out the will, including managing the estate. These provisions were clarified by the reformed inheritance law that took effect on 1 July 2019. Where an executor exists, under Civil Code Article 1013 the heirs cannot in principle dispose of the estate on their own, so the heirs' consent to the sale is in principle not required.

However, property cannot be sold to a third party while registered in the deceased's name. In practice, an inheritance registration into the heirs' names is done first, then transferred to the buyer (next section). Setting the price on an objective basis, such as a third-party appraisal, and recording the process, makes it easier to explain to the heirs and beneficiaries. The executor's duties and appointment are set out in [the duties and appointment of a will executor](https://luck428.com/en/legal/column/yuigon-shikkosha-shokumu-sennin-dare).

## In what order are the registrations needed?

In a liquidation-type bequest, registration is in principle two steps. Both are the work of a judicial scrivener (acting for the registration application); an administrative scrivener does not perform them.

| Order | Registration | Cause of registration |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | From the deceased to the heirs (all) — inheritance registration | Inheritance |
| 2 | From the heirs to the buyer — transfer of ownership | Sale |

The sale contract itself can usually be concluded between the executor and buyer without waiting for the inheritance registration to complete, but the inheritance registration must be done as a prerequisite for the transfer to the buyer. Under the obligation to register inheritance (Real Property Registration Act Article 76-2, effective 1 April 2024), an inheritance registration must in principle be applied for within three years of the day you learn both that inheritance has begun and that you have acquired ownership. Confirm the specific registration procedure with a judicial scrivener. The differences in how a will is made (holographic or notarial) are set out in [the difference between holographic and notarial wills](https://luck428.com/en/legal/column/jihitsu-kosei-yuigon).

## Whose return reports the capital gain on the sale?

This is a point often misunderstood. First, the quasi-final return is what an heir files for the deceased's own income (income from 1 January of the year of death to the date of death); under Income Tax Act Articles 124 and 125 it is submitted within four months of the day the heir learns of the start of inheritance, to the tax office for the deceased's place of tax payment. That is a return for "income the deceased earned while alive".

By contrast, who reports the capital gain arising when the executor sells the property after death is treated differently depending on the case.

| Way of viewing the tax | How the filer is organised (depends on the case) |
|---|---|
| Executor sells after inheritance begins | Because title passes through the heirs, the view is that the heirs report it as their capital gain |
| Contract while alive, settlement after death | Depending on the timing of income recognition, recognised in the deceased's quasi-final return |
| Bequest to a beneficiary or corporation | Deemed-transfer taxation or other treatment may be at issue |

The attribution of the capital gain can change with the type of bequest (specific or comprehensive), whether the beneficiary is an individual or a corporation, and how the timing of income recognition is viewed. This includes the point that, in a liquidation-type bequest, an heir may be required to file and pay even without acquiring the asset. Judgement on the tax relationship is made by a tax accountant. This article does not assert a particular conclusion.

## What clauses guard against claims for the legally secured share and unsold property?

Even in a liquidation-type bequest, the legally secured share (iryubun) of a spouse, child or other holder remains. Under Civil Code Article 1046, a holder whose secured share is infringed may claim payment of money equal to the amount of the infringement from the beneficiary and others. It stabilises execution to arrange in advance whether the distribution of proceeds can withstand such a claim, and what happens if the property is not sold or the sale is delayed.

| Guard | What is effective to include in the will |
|---|---|
| Naming the executor | Name the executor and state the power to sell and distribute proceeds |
| Purpose of liquidation | Make clear the purpose of converting to money and the distribution shares |
| If unsold | Provide for distribution in kind or re-setting if not sold within a period |
| Regard for the secured share | A distribution premised on a possible infringement claim |

## FAQ

**Q. Which is better, a liquidation-type bequest or a "give-by-inheritance" will?**
A. It cannot be said in general. If you want to leave the asset in kind, a specified-property succession will fits; if you want to distribute hard-to-divide assets as cash, a liquidation-type bequest fits. Because it varies with the asset mix, the heirs' wishes and the tax outlook, confirm the wording with an administrative scrivener, the tax with a tax accountant, and the dispute outlook with an attorney.

**Q. Can the property be transferred straight to the buyer without an inheritance registration?**
A. Property cannot be sold to a third party while in the deceased's name, so in practice an inheritance registration into the heirs' names is done first, then transferred to the buyer. These registration applications are a judicial scrivener's work. Inheritance registration is subject to the obligation that took effect on 1 April 2024, with an application duty in principle within three years.

**Q. Who pays the tax on the sale gain?**
A. Income up to the deceased's date of death is the quasi-final return (Income Tax Act Articles 124 and 125, within four months). Who reports the capital gain arising on a sale after death depends on the type of bequest and the view of the timing of income recognition. In a liquidation-type bequest, tax may fall on the heirs, and the judgement is made by a tax accountant.

**Q. Does a claim for the secured share make a liquidation-type bequest void?**
A. It does not become void. Under Civil Code Article 1046, a holder of a secured share may claim money equal to the amount of the infringement, settled out of the sale proceeds. Arranging the distribution shares and the response if the property is unsold in the will stabilises execution. Valuation and negotiation are the domain of an attorney.
