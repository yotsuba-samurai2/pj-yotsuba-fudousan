**In short:** if among the heirs there is one who is known but of unknown life or death and uncontactable, the estate-division agreement cannot proceed as things are, because an agreement is void unless made by all heirs. There are broadly two options. One is appointing an administrator of an absentee's property: where an absentee who has left their former domicile or residence and is unlikely to return readily has set up no administrator of their property, the family court, on the application of an interested party or a public prosecutor, orders the measures necessary for the administration of that property and appoints an administrator (Civil Code Article 25(1)). For this administrator to join the estate division, because it exceeds preservation, use and improvement, the family court's permission for an act beyond authority is needed (Civil Code Article 28). The other is an adjudication of disappearance: where life or death is unknown for seven years (ordinary disappearance; Civil Code Article 30(1)), or where a person who encountered war, a sunken vessel, or another peril that could cause death remains of unknown life or death for one year after the peril passed (special disappearance / peril disappearance; Article 30(2)), the family court makes the adjudication. On adjudication, in ordinary disappearance the person is deemed to have died at the expiry of the seven-year period, and in peril disappearance at the time the peril passed (Civil Code Article 31). Which to choose turns on the prospect of survival, urgency, and how you view the possibility of later reversal. This article is general information; the final judgement on which procedure is appropriate and on whether the adjudication or permission is granted rests with the family court and qualified professionals. ## Why can't the estate division proceed if there is an uncontactable heir?

An estate-division agreement comes into being only if all the heirs join and agree. An agreement made lacking some heirs is void. So if even one heir is of unknown life or death and uncontactable, the other heirs cannot decide the division among themselves, and title transfer and withdrawal of deposits also stop. Leaving it in a state of "we do not know where they are" or "we do not even know if they are alive" can let the next succession (a multi-tiered succession) pile on, increasing the parties further and becoming unmanageable.

The premise here is distinguishing whether the person "exists as an heir but is of unknown whereabouts or life and death," or whether "there is no heir at all (non-existence)." This article deals with the former: a case where the heir's existence can be confirmed by family register but one of them is of unknown life or death. The mechanism for liquidation where there is no heir is set out in [When there is no heir: the estate-liquidation administrator and a person with a special connection](https://luck428.com/en/legal/column/souzoku-nin-fuzai-seisannin-tokubetsu-enko), and the whole picture where a party has circumstances such as dementia, unknown whereabouts or minority in [When an heir has dementia, unknown whereabouts, or is a minor](https://luck428.com/en/legal/column/souzoku-ninchisho-yukuefumei-miseinen).

| Situation | Mechanism used |
|---|---|
| Heir exists but of unknown life or death | Appointment of an administrator of an absentee's property (Civil Code Article 25) / adjudication of disappearance (Article 30) |
| No heir at all (non-existence) | Estate-liquidation administrator (separate article) |
| An heir has dementia or is a minor | Adult guardianship, special representative, etc. (separate article) |

You cannot proceed treating an heir of unknown life or death as "not there." What the Civil Code provides are the two: an administrator of an absentee's property who manages the absentee's property, and an adjudication of disappearance that, after a certain period, fictionally deems death.

## When is appointing an administrator of an absentee's property suitable?

An administrator of an absentee's property is an administrator appointed by the family court where a person (an absentee) who has left their former domicile or residence and is unlikely to return readily has set up no administrator of their property. The family court may, on the application of an interested party or a public prosecutor, order the measures necessary for the administration of that property (Civil Code Article 25(1)). Other heirs who want to proceed with the division may be "interested parties." The appointed administrator, in place of the absentee, joins the estate-division agreement, so the division can be brought into being as an agreement with everyone present.

This mechanism suits cases where, whatever the life or death, you do not want to go as far as fictionally deeming death, or where you want to proceed with the division quickly. Unlike an adjudication of disappearance, you need not wait years, and you can proceed while protecting the interest of a person who may be alive. On the other hand, the administrator's remuneration and the burden of continuing to manage property corresponding to the absentee's share arise, and if the absentee appears, the administration ends. Because the administrator is not the absentee, they receive the family court's involvement (the permission for an act beyond authority, below) so that the division's content is not markedly disadvantageous to the absentee.

| Item | Content |
|---|---|
| Appointing body | Family court (application of interested party or public prosecutor; Civil Code Article 25(1)) |
| Suitable scene | You want to avoid fictionally deeming death / proceed with the division quickly |
| Administrator's position | A person managing the property in place of the absentee (not the person themselves) |
| Joining the division | Possible, but permission for an act beyond authority is needed (Civil Code Article 28) |
| Termination | Administration ends on grounds such as the absentee appearing |

Who to propose as administrator, and what circumstances to show the family court, are considered case by case. The appointment application itself is a procedure before the family court, and, as below, care is needed as to who carries it out.

## When should you choose an adjudication of disappearance (ordinary 7 years / peril 1 year)?

An adjudication of disappearance treats a person whose life or death has remained unknown as having died in law. There are two types. One is ordinary disappearance: where an absentee's life or death is unknown for seven years, the family court may, on the application of an interested party, make an adjudication of disappearance (Civil Code Article 30(1)). The other is special disappearance (peril disappearance): it is adjudicated where the life or death of a person who went to a war zone, was aboard a sunken vessel, or encountered another peril that could cause death remains unknown for one year after, respectively, the war ended, the vessel sank, or the other peril passed (Article 30(2)).

As the effect of the adjudication, the time at which death is deemed differs by type. In ordinary disappearance a person is deemed to have died at the expiry of the seven-year period, and in peril disappearance at the time the peril passed (Civil Code Article 31). If death is fictionally deemed, succession to that person as decedent opens, and the remaining heirs can proceed with the division. While it fundamentally resolves the problem of an heir of unknown life or death, it requires the lapse of a period meeting the requirement, and the procedure also takes time. If it later turns out the disappeared person was alive, the adjudication can be rescinded (the effect of rescission includes an adjustment maintaining the validity of acts done in good faith after the adjudication).

| Type | Requirement | Time deemed to have died | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ordinary disappearance | Life or death unknown for seven years | Expiry of the seven-year period | Civil Code Articles 30(1), 31 |
| Special disappearance (peril) | Life or death unknown for one year after the peril passed | Time the peril passed | Civil Code Articles 30(2), 31 |

A rough guide to choosing: if the possibility of survival remains and you do not want to go as far as deeming death, or are in a hurry, an administrator of an absentee's property; if life or death has been unknown for a long period and you want to organise it as death and fix the inheritance relations, an adjudication of disappearance. However, which is appropriate turns on the circumstances, and whether the adjudication is granted is judged by the family court.

## Why does the administrator need permission for an act beyond authority to join the division?

Even if you appoint an administrator of an absentee's property, that administrator cannot of course join the estate division freely. The administrator's authority is in principle limited to acts of preservation that maintain the current state of the property, and acts of use or improvement within a range that does not change its nature (Civil Code Article 28 provides that where the administrator needs to do an act exceeding the authority set in Civil Code Article 103 — preservation, use and improvement — they may do it with the permission of the family court). The estate division is a dispositive act fixing the attribution of the property that is the absentee's share, exceeding this range. Therefore, for the administrator to join the division agreement and agree, the family court's permission for an act beyond authority is needed.

In the examination for permission, it is seen whether the content of the division is markedly disadvantageous to the absentee. For example, content securing property corresponding to the absentee's statutory share tends to be permitted, while content unduly reducing the absentee's share tends not to be — this is the operation generally explained. In other words, the administrator is not a being who "stamps the seal in place of the absent person," but plays a role of protecting the absentee's interest under the family court's supervision.

| Act | Family court's permission |
|---|---|
| Preservation of property (maintaining the state) | Not needed (within Civil Code Article 103) |
| Use / improvement not changing the nature | Not needed (same) |
| Joining and agreeing to the estate-division agreement | Needed (permission for an act beyond authority; Civil Code Article 28) |
| Disposal such as sale of real estate | Needed (same) |

For this reason, using an administrator of an absentee's property means going through two family-court procedures: the "appointment application" and the "application for permission for an act beyond authority." Both are procedures before the family court, and the prospect of what division plan will obtain permission involves legal judgement. A scene where the grant of permission, or whether the content of the division is reasonable, is disputed is a domain an attorney handles.

## Who handles document preparation, applying as a representative, and registration?

An inheritance with an heir of unknown life or death divides by stage among different roles. Here, care is needed on the professional-boundary line. The applications for appointing an administrator of an absentee's property, for an adjudication of disappearance, and for permission for an act beyond authority are all procedures before the family court, and preparing and representing documents submitted to a court is not within an administrative scrivener's business. What the Administrative Scriveners Act makes an administrative scrivener's business is preparing documents to be submitted to public agencies and other documents on rights, duties and facts, and documents submitted to a court are not included (Administrative Scriveners Act Articles 1-2 and 1-3). That part is carried out by the person themselves or entrusted to an attorney (or, for document preparation, a judicial scrivener).

What an administrative scrivener can handle is the groundwork of the inheritance and the agreement document after the procedure is fixed. Concretely: collecting family registers to fix the scope of heirs, investigating heirs, and preparing a chart of inheritance relations; and, where agreement of all heirs is reached through the participation of the administrator of an absentee's property (with permission for an act beyond authority) or through a fixed adjudication of disappearance, supporting preparation of the estate-division agreement. The investigation premised on assembling the parties to the agreement, and drawing up the document after agreement is reached, are an administrative scrivener's role.

## FAQ

**Q. Even if one heir is of unknown life or death, can the remaining heirs divide the estate?**
A. No. An estate-division agreement is void unless made by all heirs. Where there is an heir of unknown life or death, you must go through either appointing an administrator of an absentee's property (Civil Code Article 25(1)) and adding that administrator to the agreement, or obtaining an adjudication of disappearance (Civil Code Article 30) and organising it as death. Which is appropriate turns on the prospect of survival and urgency.

**Q. If I appoint an administrator of an absentee's property, can they join the division at once?**
A. Appointment alone is not enough. The administrator's authority is in principle limited to preservation, use and improvement (Civil Code Article 103), and the estate division exceeds this. To join and agree to the agreement, the family court's permission for an act beyond authority is needed (Civil Code Article 28). In the examination it is seen whether the content is markedly disadvantageous to the absentee.

**Q. After how many years can an adjudication of disappearance be applied for?**
A. Ordinary disappearance can be requested of the family court where life or death has been unknown for seven years (Civil Code Article 30(1)). By contrast, special disappearance (peril disappearance) — on encountering a war zone, a sunken vessel, or another peril that could cause death — suffices if life or death is unknown for one year after the peril passed (Article 30(2)). The time deemed to be death is the expiry of seven years for ordinary disappearance, and the time the peril passed for peril disappearance (Article 31).

**Q. Can an administrative scrivener prepare the application for an adjudication of disappearance?**
A. An application to the family court is a document submitted to a court, and its preparation and representation are not within an administrative scrivener's business (Administrative Scriveners Act Articles 1-2, 1-3). That part is carried out by the person themselves or entrusted to an attorney (for document preparation, a judicial scrivener). What an administrative scrivener can support is collecting family registers, investigating heirs, and preparing the estate-division agreement where agreement of all is reached after the procedure is fixed.
