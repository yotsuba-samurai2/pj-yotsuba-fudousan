**In short:** a secret (sealed) will keeps the will's content secret from everyone, including the notary, while a notary and two or more witnesses certify that the will exists and is the testator's own (Civil Code Article 970). Its form has four parts: (1) the testator signs and seals the document; (2) the testator seals the envelope with the same seal used on the document; (3) the testator submits the sealed envelope before one notary and two or more witnesses and states that it is their own will and the writer's name and address; and (4) the notary records the date of submission and the statement on the envelope and signs and seals it together with the testator and the witnesses (Civil Code Article 970(1), items 1–4). The body need not be handwritten — it may be word-processed or written by a third party — which is a major difference from the holographic will (Article 968), which requires the whole text in the testator's hand (the signature on the document, however, is made by the testator). The notary's fee is a flat 13,000 yen regardless of the value of the property (Notary Fee Order Article 28; accessed 2026-11-24). Even if something is lacking in the secret form, it is valid as a holographic will if it meets the holographic form (whole text handwritten, date, signature, seal) (Civil Code Article 971). After the inheritance opens, the family court's probate is required (Civil Code Article 1004). This article is general information and does not reach the final judgement of whether a particular will is valid. ## What is a secret will?

A secret will is one of the ordinary forms usable at any time in normal circumstances (holographic, notarial, secret), and it serves the situation where "I want to keep the content secret, yet secure that the will exists and has not been altered." The notary does not see the contents of the envelope. What the notary and the two or more witnesses certify is the fact that the person stated "this is my own will," the writer's name and address, and the date of submission to the notary — they do not touch the content itself. Hence the position of "content secret, existence notarised."

| Point of comparison | Holographic will (Art. 968) | Notarial will (Art. 969) | Secret will (Art. 970) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Does anyone know the content | No one (content stays private even under the custody system) | The notary and witnesses know | Not even the notary or witnesses know (content secret) |
| Notary's involvement | None (the custody system is at the Legal Affairs Bureau) | The notary draws it up | The notary certifies existence and date (does not see the content) |
| Is the body handwritten | Whole text handwritten (except a property inventory) | Not needed (the notary draws it up) | Not needed (word-processed or written by another is fine) |

Not having to handwrite the text yourself suits those with impaired hands or where there is much to write. On the other hand, because the notary does not check the content, there is a risk that a defect in form or content goes unnoticed. The difference between the ordinary holographic and notarial wills themselves is set out in [The difference between a holographic will and a notarial will](https://luck428.com/en/legal/column/jihitsu-kosei-yuigon).

## What must be met under the form (Art. 970)?

A secret will takes no effect unless it follows all four of the forms set out in Civil Code Article 970(1). If even one is lacking, it is void as a secret will (though there is room for conversion under Article 971, discussed below).

| Order | Form | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| (1) | The testator signs the document and affixes a seal | Civil Code Art. 970(1) item 1 |
| (2) | The testator seals the document and seals the envelope with the same seal used on the document | Civil Code Art. 970(1) item 2 |
| (3) | The testator submits the sealed envelope before one notary and two or more witnesses and states that it is their own will and the writer's name and address | Civil Code Art. 970(1) item 3 |
| (4) | The notary records on the envelope the date of submission and the testator's statement, then signs and seals it together with the testator and the witnesses | Civil Code Art. 970(1) item 4 |

The seal in (2) must use the same seal as the one affixed to the document; sealing the envelope with a different seal lacks the form. The "writer" in (3) is the person who wrote (input and printed) the body of the document, and may be the testator or a third party. The form for additions and alterations to the document applies as for the holographic will (Civil Code Article 970(2) applies Article 968(3)). Witnesses also have disqualifications: a minor, a presumptive heir or legatee and their spouse and lineal relatives, and the notary's spouse, relatives within the fourth degree, clerk, and employee cannot be witnesses (Civil Code Article 974). Where a person unable to speak makes a secret will, there is a special rule to substitute a statement through an interpreter, or a statement written by hand on the envelope, for the statement in Article 970(1) item 3 (Civil Code Article 972).

## How do you choose between it, the holographic will, and the notarial will?

The three ordinary forms differ in secrecy, cost, whether probate is needed, and the risk of defects. On cost, the notary's fee for a secret will is a flat 13,000 yen regardless of the value of the property (Notary Fee Order Article 28; accessed 2026-11-24). A notarial will incurs a fee according to the value of the target property. Probate (the family-court procedure to confirm the will after the inheritance opens) is not needed for a notarial will but is needed for a secret will (Civil Code Article 1004).

| Point | Holographic will (Art. 968) | Notarial will (Art. 969) | Secret will (Art. 970) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Secrecy of content | Kept | Known to the notary and witnesses | Kept (the notary does not see it) |
| Notary's fee | None | Sliding scale by property value | Flat 13,000 yen (Art. 28) |
| Witnesses | Not needed | Two or more needed | Two or more needed |
| Family court's probate | Needed (not needed under the custody system) | Not needed | Needed (Art. 1004) |
| Main risk | Defects in the handwritten text, loss, alteration | Cost; content conveyed to third parties | Defects in form or content go unnoticed |

A wish for "no one to know the content, but the notary to secure existence and anti-alteration" fits the secret will. However, because the notary does not check the content, unease over the will's effect tends to remain, and in practice the notarial will is often chosen. If you value a reliable form where the notary checks the content too, [Making a notarial will and the notary's fee](https://luck428.com/en/legal/column/kosei-shosho-yuigon-koshonin-shonin-hiyo) is a useful reference. This article is general information, and which form is best depends on the individual circumstances.

## What happens if there is a defect in form? (Art. 971, probate)

If even one of the secret will's forms is lacking, it is void as a secret will. However, Civil Code Article 971 provides relief: even if something is lacking in the Article 970 form, a secret will is valid as a holographic will where it meets the holographic form (whole text handwritten, date, signature, seal) (Civil Code Article 971). This is called "conversion into a holographic will." Note that a secret will is often made with the body word-processed or written by another, and in that case it does not meet the holographic "whole text handwritten" requirement, so it cannot be converted and may end up void.

Once the inheritance opens, a will with a seal on it must not be opened freely: it must be opened at the family court in the presence of the heirs or their agents, and probate must be obtained (Civil Code Article 1004). Probate is a procedure to confirm the shape and state of the will and prevent forgery or alteration; it does not guarantee that the content of the will is valid. Whether the form is met, and whether the content issued from the testator's true intention, are ultimately judged by a qualified professional or the court on the individual facts. The effect of the will's content itself and disputes over the reserved portion are in the attorney's field.

## FAQ

**Q. In a secret will, does the notary read the content of the will?**
A. No. A secret will is made by submitting the sealed document before one notary and two or more witnesses and stating that it is one's own will and the writer's name and address; what the notary certifies goes only to existence, the date of submission, and the fact of the statement (Civil Code Article 970). The content is inside the sealed envelope, and neither the notary nor the witnesses see it. That is exactly how the content stays secret — but the flip side is that the notary cannot notice a defect in the content.

**Q. May the body be prepared on a computer?**
A. Yes. The body of a secret will need not be handwritten; it may be word-processed or written by a third party. The signature on the document, however, is made by the testator, who affixes a seal (Civil Code Article 970(1) item 1). This is the major difference from the holographic will (Civil Code Article 968), which requires the whole text in the testator's hand.

**Q. How much does it cost?**
A. The notary's fee is a flat 13,000 yen regardless of the value of the target property (Notary Fee Order Article 28; accessed 2026-11-24). This differs in calculation from the notarial will, whose fee slides by property value. Separately, out-of-pocket costs such as a gratuity where witnesses are engaged externally may arise.

**Q. If there is a defect in form, is it entirely void?**
A. It becomes void as a secret will, but if it meets the holographic will's form (whole text handwritten, date, signature, seal), it is valid as a holographic will (Civil Code Article 971). However, if the body was word-processed it does not meet "whole text handwritten" and cannot be converted. The final judgement of whether the form is met is made by a qualified professional or the family court on the individual facts, each as an independent business under a separate contract.
