**In short:** A kinship notarial certificate issued by a mainland Chinese notary office is one document that can help show a family relationship in a Japanese inheritance procedure. It does not by itself establish everyone entitled to inherit. Before obtaining documents, ask the receiving institution in Japan which relationships and originals it needs and whether it requires a Japanese translation or an apostille. This guide covers preparing documents and illustrates renvoi only for intestate succession to real property in Japan. It does not decide the law or whether documents will be accepted in an individual case.

## Where an heir is in mainland China, where does the Japanese inheritance procedure get stuck?

Japan and mainland China use different documents to show family relationships and authenticate a person's signature. The mainland household register is not the same as Japan's family register. Use this table to identify possible documents to ask about, not as a list of documents accepted for every Japanese procedure.

| What to show | Examples of evidence from mainland China |
| --- | --- |
| Family relationship | A kinship notarial certificate from a Chinese notary office, among other evidence |
| Authenticity of a signature | Notarisation of the signature, where required |
| Address | Notarial evidence of address or other accepted documents |

A Japanese bank and the Legal Affairs Bureau can ask for different documents. Before arranging a certificate, confirm whose relationships must be shown and which records the recipient needs. For the broader process, see [inheritance involving overseas heirs](https://luck428.com/en/legal/column/souzoku-kaigai-gaikokuseki).

## What can a kinship notarial certificate show?

A mainland Chinese notary office certifies the relationships described in the document. Ask that office which household register, identification and supporting records it requires. A Japanese recipient may need additional records relating to the deceased or other family members. A kinship certificate is not, on its own, a determination of eligibility or a complete list of heirs. Our office does not perform notarisation in China.

## Does the certificate need consular legalisation or an apostille?

For public documents covered by the Apostille Convention, which took effect between China and Japan on 7 November 2023, an apostille generally replaces consular legalisation when authentication is needed. It is issued by China's Ministry of Foreign Affairs or an authorised local foreign affairs office. An apostille authenticates the origin of a document, such as its signature or seal; it does not verify its substantive claims or guarantee acceptance in Japan. Before obtaining the documents, check which originals, translations and apostilles the receiving institution actually requires.

## How do you arrange signatures and powers of attorney for the estate-division agreement from China?

Before signing an estate division agreement or a power of attorney in mainland China, ask the recipient in Japan which form it accepts, who must sign and which acts may be delegated. Then, as required, arrange local notarisation of the signature and any apostille or Japanese translation. Some procedures can be arranged without travelling to Japan, but this does not guarantee that appearance will never be required or that the documents will be accepted. Give the signatory a translation or explanation so they understand any Japanese-language document they sign.

## Who decides the governing law (which country's inheritance law divides the estate)?

Article 36 of Japan's Act on General Rules for Application of Laws starts from the deceased's national law. For example, if that refers to Chinese law and the matter concerns **intestate succession** to real property located in Japan, Article 31 of China's Act on the Application of Laws to Foreign-Related Civil Relations refers to the law where the real property is situated. This may refer the matter back to Japanese law through renvoi under Article 41 of the Japanese Act. Do not assume the same outcome for deposits or wills. Consult a qualified professional, such as a lawyer, to determine the governing law and scope of heirs in a particular case.

## FAQ

**Q. Can someone in Japan obtain the kinship notarial certificate on my behalf?**
A. The certificate is issued by a notary office in mainland China. Our office does not carry out notarisation there, but can help organise evidence for the Japanese recipient and prepare translations and supporting documents. The certificate alone does not establish all heirs.

**Q. Do I need both an apostille and consular legalisation?**
A. When authentication of a covered public document is needed, an apostille generally replaces consular legalisation; both are not required by the Convention. Confirm in advance whether the Japanese recipient requires the apostille itself or a Japanese translation.

**Q. Must all heirs come to Japan to divide the estate?**
A. Not necessarily. Confirm the recipient's accepted form and any signature, notarisation, apostille or translation requirements in advance. Some matters can proceed remotely, but the recipient decides what is accepted.

**Q. If the deceased was a Chinese national, is the estate always divided under Chinese law?**
A. Not automatically. In intestate succession to Japanese real property, the Chinese conflict rule may point back to Japanese law. Do not carry that result over to bank deposits or wills. Consult a qualified professional for a case-specific determination.
