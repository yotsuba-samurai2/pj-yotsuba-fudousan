## What makes an encroachment a problem for the sale of inherited real estate?

The encroachment itself does not bar a sale. The problems are the need to explain it as an important matter so the buyer does not "buy without knowing," and that the buyer and the buyer's lender (a financial institution) care about the outlook for future correction.

An encroachment can be yours—a fence, eaves or tree branches extending into the neighbour's land—or the neighbour's, extending into yours. In an inherited home, it often turns up as a hedge that has grown over the line over the years, an old fence straddling the boundary, or eaves projecting over the neighbour's side.

| Type of encroachment | What matters for the sale |
|---|---|
| Branch / root over the line | Handling under amended Civil Code art. 233 / art. 234. Branches, in principle, "have them cut"; roots you may cut |
| Fence / retaining-wall encroachment | Ownership and the boundary position; overlaps with [selling land with a retaining wall (Japanese)](https://luck428.com/en/column/gake-yoheki-tochi-baikyaku-bunkyo) |
| Eaves / part of a building over the line | Building near the boundary (Civil Code art. 234) and correction at rebuild |
| Boundary itself undetermined | The encroachment cannot be fixed; survey and confirming the lot line come first |

When a real-estate broker brokers a sale, it explains, in the important-matters explanation under Real Estate Brokerage Act (Act No. 176 of 1952) art. 35, matters that materially affect the transaction decision. Whether there is an encroachment, its content, and whether a correction promise exists tie directly to the buyer's use of the land and future burden, so it is practice to explain them. We check the current state of the encroachment on site and from records, and reflect it accurately in the important-matters explanation.

## Under amended Civil Code art. 233, how far can you cut a neighbour's branch?

The principle is still "have the owner of the neighbour's tree cut the overhanging branch." The amendment in force from 1 April 2023 allows the encroached-on side to cut the branch itself only in limited cases. Roots you may cut yourself, as before.

The framework of Civil Code art. 233 (amended by Act No. 24 of 2021; in force 1 April 2023) is as follows.

| Paragraph | Content |
|---|---|
| Art. 233 (1) | Where a neighbour's tree branch crosses the boundary, you may have the tree's owner cut the branch (principle) |
| Art. 233 (2) | Where the tree is co-owned, each co-owner may cut the overhanging branch |
| Art. 233 (3) | You may cut the branch yourself where: ① you demanded the owner cut it but they do not within a reasonable period; ② you cannot know the owner or their whereabouts; or ③ there is an urgent circumstance |
| Art. 233 (4) | Where a neighbour's tree root crosses the boundary, you may cut the root |

Before the amendment, for branches you had to "have them cut" or, if refused, sue, obtain a judgment and enforce it. The principle is unchanged after the amendment: only when one of "demand and wait a reasonable period," "owner unknown," or "urgent" applies may you cut it yourself (para. 3). The reasonable period depends on the case, but some municipal explanations give about two weeks as an example. Cutting on your own judgment can instead expose you to a damages claim, so judging which requirement applies is a lawyer's field. Where part of a building (eaves, etc.) encroaches, Civil Code art. 234—requiring a building to be set back at least 50 cm from the boundary—is relevant, and a claim to correct a violation is subject to a time limit (damages only after one year from start of work or after completion; art. 234 (2)). We do not assert whether cutting or correction is permissible.

## What should the memorandum on encroachment say?

Where an encroachment cannot be resolved at once, it is practice to exchange a "memorandum on encroachment (confirmation)" with the neighbouring owner—confirming the current state, promising correction at a future rebuild/renovation, and binding the buyer (a third party) to that promise.

A memorandum generally includes the following (it varies with the circumstances).

| Item | What to write |
|---|---|
| Identifying the encroachment | Which thing (fence, branch, eaves) crosses, from which side and in what range; identified by survey drawing/photos |
| Mutual confirmation of status | That removal will not be demanded immediately in the current state |
| Future correction | That the encroachment will be resolved at the chance of a rebuild/renovation/tree renewal |
| Succession | That the memorandum binds even if the owner changes (by sale or inheritance) |
| Cost-bearing | Who bears the correction cost |

Such a memorandum is work an administrative scrivener prepares as a document concerning rights and duties. Where the parties are in conflict over the fact of the encroachment or the need for correction, it can become negotiation, mediation or litigation—a lawyer's field. ## When the boundary is undetermined, who do you ask for what?

Whether and how far there is an encroachment cannot be fixed without knowing where the boundary (lot line) is. Surveying and confirming the lot line is the exclusive field of a land and house surveyor. Completing a boundary-confirmation survey or lot-line determination before selling makes the encroachment explanation and the memorandum reliable.

The Land and House Surveyor Act (Act No. 228 of 1950) makes the surveying related to a land's lot line (the public-law boundary), registration of land/building descriptions, and agency in lot-line-determination procedures the business of a land and house surveyor. There is a "confirmation survey" that confirms the boundary with the neighbouring owner present and produces a confirmed survey drawing, and a "lot-line-determination system" by which the Legal Affairs Bureau (the lot-line registrar) clarifies the lot line when it is unclear.

| Trouble | Who to ask |
|---|---|
| Survey and produce a confirmed survey drawing; check boundary markers | Land and house surveyor |
| Apply for lot-line determination where the line is unclear | Land and house surveyor (agency) |
| Prepare a memorandum/agreement on encroachment | Administrative scrivener |
| Dispute over the boundary/encroachment with the neighbour (negotiation, mediation, litigation) | Lawyer |
| Inheritance registration; registration of a building description | Judicial scrivener / land and house surveyor |

With the boundary determined, the area and position of the encroachment can be worked into the important-matters explanation and the memorandum. Where a private-road share or access is involved, see [selling inherited land with a private-road share (Japanese)](https://luck428.com/en/column/souzoku-shido-mochibun-ichitei-doro-baikyaku); for the flow from a boundary-confirmation survey to a sale, [how to proceed from a boundary-confirmation survey to the sale of inherited real estate (Japanese)](https://luck428.com/en/column/souzoku-fudosan-kyoukai-kakutei-sokuryo-baikyaku). We check existing survey drawings and the registration status, and help with the outlook on whether a confirmation survey or lot-line determination is needed and the referral to a land and house surveyor.

## How can you sell with the encroachment left, and how does it affect the price?

There is a way of resolving the encroachment before selling and a way of selling with the encroachment left, with a memorandum attached. Which to choose depends on the difficulty, cost and time of correction and on how the buyer and lender take it. The price can be affected by the estimated correction cost and the buyer's trouble.

| How to sell | Point |
|---|---|
| Resolve the encroachment, then sell | Rebuild the fence, cut the branch, alter the eaves to remove the encroachment; needs cost and the neighbour's cooperation |
| Leave the encroachment, sell with a memorandum | Sell as-is, with a memorandum promising future correction passed to the buyer; sells well if the explanation and document are in order |
| Sell with the boundary/encroachment still disputed | Price tends to fall and buyers/financing are hard to get; resolving the dispute first is the right order |

Where the buyer uses a mortgage, the financial institution may check the outlook for correction (whether a memorandum exists). If the memorandum is in order, it is not unusual for the transaction to proceed even with the encroachment left. How much the price is affected varies with the content of the encroachment, the correction cost and the location, and is assessed individually in the appraisal. We take consultations from comparing "sell after resolving" and "sell as-is with a memorandum" and the arrangement of who to ask for what in each. ## Frequently asked questions

**Q. May I cut a branch that has grown over from the neighbour's land?**
A. The principle is to have the tree's owner cut the overhanging branch (Civil Code art. 233 (1)). The amendment in force from 1 April 2023 lets you cut it yourself where: ① you demanded the owner cut it but they do not within a reasonable period; ② you cannot know the owner or their whereabouts; or ③ there is an urgent circumstance (art. 233 (3)). Cutting without meeting a requirement can expose you to a damages claim, and judging which applies is a lawyer's field. A root over the line you may cut yourself, as before (art. 233 (4)).

**Q. Does an encroachment mean the inherited home cannot be sold?**
A. It can be sold. The encroachment itself does not bar a sale. In practice, the existence and content of the encroachment is explained to the buyer as an important matter, and an encroachment that cannot be resolved at once is covered by a "memorandum on encroachment" with the neighbour promising future correction and passed to the buyer. If the memorandum is in order, the transaction often proceeds even with the encroachment left. We handle the explanation and the documentation; an administrative scrivener prepares the memorandum.

**Q. Who prepares the memorandum on encroachment?**
A. As a document concerning rights and duties, it is work an administrative scrivener prepares. The memorandum includes identifying the encroachment, mutual confirmation of the current state, a promise to correct at a future rebuild/renovation, binding a successor even if the owner changes, and cost-bearing. Where the parties are in conflict over the fact or the correction, it can become negotiation, mediation or litigation—a lawyer's field. We arrange the current state and the boundary records underlying the memorandum and connect you to an administrative scrivener/lawyer.

**Q. When the boundary is unclear, what should I start with?**
A. Surveying and confirming the boundary (lot line) is the exclusive field of a land and house surveyor (Land and House Surveyor Act). There is a confirmation survey done with the neighbour present, and a lot-line-determination system by which the Legal Affairs Bureau clarifies the line when it is unclear. With the boundary determined, the range of the encroachment can be shown by measurement in the important-matters explanation and the memorandum. We check existing survey drawings and the registration status and help with the outlook on whether a confirmation survey or lot-line determination is needed and the referral to a land and house surveyor.
