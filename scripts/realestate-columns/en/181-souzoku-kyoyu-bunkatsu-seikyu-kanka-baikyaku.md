## When you cannot sell by discussion, what can the partition claim choose?

Where the co-owners cannot reach agreement on partition, or cannot discuss it, the partition may be claimed from the court (Civil Code Art. 258(1)). As a rule, each co-owner may claim partition at any time (Art. 256(1); though a no-partition contract not exceeding five years is possible). So there is a way to unravel deadlocks like "I want to sell but the other co-owners won't agree" or "I can't reach them" through court procedure.

But a threshold distinction is needed first. If the inherited property is still "undivided co-ownership" that has not gone through estate division, then as a rule it is divided not by partition of co-owned property (Art. 258) but by the family court's estate division (agreement, mediation, adjudication; Art. 907). This is because, where all or a share of a co-owned thing belongs to the estate and the co-heirs are to divide the estate, partition under Art. 258 cannot be made for that share (Art. 258-2(1)). As an exception, once ten years have passed from the start of inheritance, partition under Art. 258 can be made even for a share belonging to the estate (Art. 258-2(2); unless an heir objects). Telling undivided co-ownership from ordinary co-ownership first is the starting point for choosing the procedure.

| Situation | Procedure used | Provision |
|---|---|---|
| Still undivided co-ownership (within 10 years of inheritance) | Family court estate division | Arts. 907, 258-2(1) |
| Ordinary co-ownership after division / a third party acquires a share | District court partition of co-owned property | Art. 258 |
| Ten years passed from the start of inheritance | Partition of co-owned property also available (objection exception) | Art. 258-2(2) |

## Among partition in kind, compensation and conversion to cash, how does the court prioritise?

In judicial partition of co-owned property, the methods the court may order are set out in order in the statute. First, partition in kind of the co-owned thing (Art. 258(2)(i)), or making a co-owner bear a debt to acquire all or part of the other co-owners' shares (compensation partition / full price compensation; item (ii)). Then, where partition by these methods is impossible, or would risk markedly reducing the value, the court may order its auction (conversion to cash; Art. 258(3)).

So on the statute, partition in kind and compensation partition are considered first, and only when those are difficult does it proceed to auction (conversion to cash) as a last resort. A single building or hard-to-divide land is hard to divide in kind; if a particular co-owner has means, compensation partition; if not and it cannot be divided in kind, conversion to cash — such a flow tends to arise. In the partition judgment, the court may also order payment of money, delivery of things, performance of a registration obligation, and other performance (Art. 258(4)). For the practice where a particular heir pays compensation to acquire, see [compensation partition: acquiring property and paying compensation](https://luck428.com/en/column/souzoku-fudosan-daisho-bunkatsu-hyoka-baikyaku).

| Method | Content | Provision |
|---|---|---|
| Partition in kind | Divide the co-owned thing itself | Art. 258(2)(i) |
| Compensation partition (full price compensation) | One acquires and pays compensation to others | Art. 258(2)(ii) |
| Conversion to cash (auction) | Sell and divide the proceeds | Art. 258(3) |

## If it becomes conversion to cash, does a negotiated sale net more than a formal auction?

Conversion to cash (sell and divide the money) has two routes: a formal auction through court procedure, and a negotiated sale by the co-owners on the market. The practical view is that a negotiated sale sells at a price closer to market value and nets more, because an auction is by bidding and buyers tend to hold the price down to price in risk. But a negotiated sale needs the co-owners' cooperation, so a fierce dispute may push it to a formal auction.

A formal auction sells through court procedure on the basis of a judgment ordering the auction under Art. 258(3). It is easier to foresee the price and procedure but tends to be cheaper than ordinary market circulation (the actual successful-bid rate and price vary greatly by case; unverified). A negotiated sale, even before judgment, is a sale by the parties on the market as a court settlement or as an agreement in the course of the partition claim. It has the advantage that the co-owners can choose price and timing, so if agreement can be reached, it tends to maximise the net proceeds. For the general flow of selling to divide the money by agreement, see [the flow of conversion-to-cash division by agreement](https://luck428.com/en/column/kanka-bunkatsu-fudosan-baikyaku-nagare). Because which is better varies by property, timing and buyer pool, do not assert it — compare both estimates to decide.

| Method of conversion | Price tendency | How it proceeds |
|---|---|---|
| Formal auction | Tends to be cheaper than market (tendency; unverified) | Court procedure; proceeds even without the co-owners' cooperation |
| Negotiated sale | Closer to market price, nets more (tendency) | Presupposes the co-owners' agreement/settlement; choose price and timing |

## Frequently asked questions

**Q. The other co-owners won't agree to sell our inherited property. Is there a way to sell?**
A. Yes. Where talks cannot be settled, you can claim partition of co-owned property from the court (Civil Code Art. 258(1)). As a rule, each co-owner may claim partition at any time (Art. 256). But while it is still undivided co-ownership, the rule is the family court's estate division. Telling undivided co-ownership from ordinary co-ownership is the starting point.

**Q. Why can't you claim partition of co-owned property while it is undivided co-ownership?**
A. Because where all or a share of a co-owned thing belongs to the estate and the co-heirs are to divide it, partition under Art. 258 cannot be made for that share, and it is divided by the family court's estate division (Art. 258-2(1)). As an exception, once ten years have passed from the start of inheritance, partition of co-owned property also becomes available (Art. 258-2(2); unless an objection is filed).

**Q. Which method of partition does the court choose?**
A. On the statute, partition in kind or compensation partition (full price compensation) is considered first (Art. 258(2)), and only when it cannot be so divided or would risk markedly reducing the value is auction (conversion to cash) ordered (Art. 258(3)). Hard-to-divide property like a building tends to end in compensation partition or conversion to cash. The final decision is the court's.

**Q. Between auction and negotiated sale, which nets more?**
A. In general, a negotiated sale on the market fetches a higher price and nets more than an auction, because an auction prices risk into the bid and tends to be cheaper. But the actual price gap varies by property, timing and buyer pool (unverified). A negotiated sale needs the co-owners' agreement, so estimate both and compare.
