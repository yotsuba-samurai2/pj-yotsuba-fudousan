## What does "with disclosure matters" (a psychological defect) mean?

"With disclosure matters" is a label indicating not a physical defect (a leak, termites, etc.) but a circumstance that has a material effect on the judgment of the buyer or renter — in particular, the fact that a person has died at the property in the past (a so-called psychological defect) — which the seller's side must disclose. The Real Estate Brokerage Act prohibits, towards the other party to a transaction and the like, the act of deliberately failing to tell the truth or telling an untruth about the quality of the land or building, the terms of the transaction and other matters that would have a material effect on the other party's judgment (Art. 47(i)). Together with the important-matters explanation (Art. 35), it is not permitted to conceal a circumstance such as a person's death and have the party conclude the contract.

Not every "death of a person" is subject to disclosure, however. As a guide to the interpretation of what should be disclosed and until when, MLIT established, on 8 October 2021, the "Guideline on the disclosure of a person's death by real estate brokers." This sets out the interpretation of the duty that a broker owes under the Real Estate Brokerage Act regarding the treatment of a psychological defect in a real estate transaction; it is not a statute itself but a practical criterion for judgment. What a Chinese-speaking buyer should first grasp is that "with disclosure matters" is not the defect itself but the disclosure of information for judgment, and that the treatment differs between a rental and a sale.

| Category | Content | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Physical defect | A physical defect such as a leak or equipment failure | Non-conformity liability (Civil Code) |
| Psychological defect | A past death of a person and other circumstances with a material effect on judgment | Brokerage Act Arts. 35, 47(i) |
| Guide for judgment | Whether and how far a person's death must be disclosed | MLIT guideline (established 2021) |

## Under the MLIT guideline, over how many years and how far is disclosure made?

The guideline says that a natural death at the property, or a death from an accident in the course of daily life such as a fall, choking or an accident while bathing, need not in principle be disclosed (though where the body was left for a long period and special cleaning and the like were carried out, the treatment differs). By contrast, for a homicide or suicide, or a death involving special cleaning and the like, in a rental transaction the guide is that after roughly three years have passed from the occurrence of the matter (or, where special cleaning and the like were carried out, from its discovery), it need not in principle be disclosed.

For a sale transaction, on the other hand, no such period-based exemption like "roughly three years" is shown. In a sale, regardless of the period that has passed since the death or the cause of death, where the buyer asks or where the broker recognises that there is a special circumstance the buyer should grasp given the magnitude of the social impact, it must be disclosed. In other words, for a Chinese-speaking buyer who "buys" real estate, whether for investment or own use, the most important point is that in a sale there is no mechanism by which disclosure automatically disappears with the passage of time. Note too that a matter with a particularly large degree of criminality or social impact may fall outside the three-year guide for rentals.

| Type of death | Treatment in a rental | Treatment in a sale |
|---|---|---|
| Natural death / accidental death in daily life | In principle need not be disclosed | In principle need not be disclosed |
| Homicide / suicide / death involving special cleaning | In principle unnecessary after roughly three years from occurrence (special cleaning: discovery) | No time limit |
| Matter with large criminality / social impact | May need disclosure even after three years | Same |

## Is disclosure still required for a tenanted or a demolished property?

In an owner change of an income property (buying a tenanted property), even if there is a current tenant, if a person died in that unit or the property in the past, disclosure to the buyer of the sale is judged by the guide for sales. Whether "roughly three years" had passed under the previous lease does not automatically govern whether disclosure is needed in the new sale. Because a sale has no time limit, the arrangement that the seller does not disclose on the ground that three years have passed under the rental does not, in principle, hold. As a buyer, you need to confirm the history of past move-ins and move-outs and whether special cleaning was done, by inquiry to the seller and the management company.

Where the building is demolished and the land is sold as vacant land, whether the psychological defect remains attached to the land is judged by the individual circumstances, and it is not that disclosure automatically becomes unnecessary because it was demolished. In a matter with a large social impact, there may be a case where, even in a land transaction after the building has been removed, it should be disclosed as a circumstance affecting the buyer's judgment. Even where disclosure is made, the guideline requires full consideration for the honour and the peace of life of the deceased and their bereaved family, and sets out a framework of consideration under which what is disclosed is limited to the time of occurrence of the matter (or, where special cleaning and the like were carried out, the time of discovery), the place, the cause of death (or, if unknown, that fact), and that special cleaning and the like were carried out — there is no need to disclose the name, age, address, family composition, the specific manner of death, the circumstances of discovery, and so on.

| Situation | How disclosure is considered |
|---|---|
| Owner change of a tenanted property | Judged by the sale guide (the rental three-year passage does not automatically apply to a sale) |
| Sale of vacant land after demolition | Not automatically unnecessary because of demolition; judged individually |
| Content when disclosing | Limited to time, place, cause of death, and whether special cleaning; name etc. not disclosed |

## Which documents should a Chinese-speaking buyer check before the contract?

In the practice of the buyer's DD, assemble the following documents before the contract and check their content: the important-matters explanation (Art. 35 of the Brokerage Act — whether and what disclosure matters concerning a person's death are stated); the property-condition report and disclosure statement filled in by the seller or lessor (whether there was a past death of a person or special cleaning); and, if tenanted, the lease agreement, the rent roll and the move-in/move-out history. The guideline proceeds on the premise that a broker may, in principle, confirm by the method of asking the seller or lessor to enter the information in a disclosure statement and the like, and does not impose a duty of voluntary investigation such as questioning neighbours. The information the seller or lessor discloses in the disclosure statement is therefore the starting point for confirmation.

## Frequently asked questions

**Q. Should I always avoid a property "with disclosure matters"?**
A. It cannot be said uniformly. "With disclosure matters" is not a physical defect itself but the disclosure of a circumstance that has a material effect on the buyer's judgment (often a past death of a person). The evaluation changes with how it is priced in and the use (investment or own residence). Confirm whether and how far there is disclosure in the important-matters explanation; a valuation discount lies with a real estate appraiser and a tax accountant, and a dispute with a lawyer. Yotsuba Real Estate Co., Ltd. handles up to organising the content of the documents.

**Q. Once three years pass in a rental, does disclosure also become unnecessary in a sale?**
A. No. The "roughly three years" guide in the MLIT guideline is for a rental transaction, and no period-based exemption is shown for a sale transaction. In a sale, regardless of period or cause of death, where the buyer asks or the broker recognises a special circumstance given the magnitude of the social impact, it must be disclosed. The arrangement of not disclosing in a sale on the ground that three years have passed in a rental does not, in principle, hold.

**Q. Can a natural death ever be disclosed?**
A. A natural death at the property, or an accidental death in the course of daily life, need not in principle be disclosed. However, where the body was left for a long period and special cleaning and the like were carried out, the treatment differs and it may become subject to disclosure. Also, where the buyer asks, it must be disclosed whether in a sale or a rental. The individual judgment requires confirmation by a real estate transaction specialist.

**Q. Will the real estate company investigate disclosure matters on its own?**
A. The guideline proceeds on the premise that a broker may, in principle, confirm by asking the seller or lessor to enter the information in a disclosure statement and the like, and does not impose a duty of voluntary investigation such as questioning neighbours. The information the seller or lessor discloses is therefore the starting point. You can request an additional inquiry to the seller's side on points of concern.
