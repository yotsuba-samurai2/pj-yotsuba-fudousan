## Why is land hard to sell as is when an akamichi/aomichi (an extra-legal public object) is on the site?

An akamichi (footpath / red line) or aomichi (waterway / blue line) is the trace of an old footpath or waterway, shown as a red or blue line on the Legal Affairs Bureau's cadastral map. Because they are not governed by special laws such as the Road Act or the River Act, they are called "extra-legal public objects", and their owner is in many cases the municipality that manages the land (those that have lost their function, the State = a Finance Bureau).

If an akamichi/waterway cuts into inherited land, that part is not the heir's and cannot be freely used as site. For building or subdivision plans too, the site cannot be used as a whole, affecting road access and the effective building-land area. From a buyer's or a bank's view, it is a "site with a strip of land under someone else's name inside it", which hampers the sale.

| State | Buyer's worry |
|---|---|
| An akamichi/waterway cuts into the site | That part cannot be used as site, making building/use plans hard |
| The site abuts an akamichi/waterway | Road access and boundary treatment unclear, valuation tends to fall |
| The cadastral map has a red/blue line but there is no trace on site | Current state and the map differ; a boundary/ownership survey is needed |

In practice you use-abolish this extra-legal public object, take a sell-off, fold it into the site, and then sell. ## In what order, and to where, do I apply for use-abolition and a sell-off?

To fold an extra-legal public object into the site, you first have the municipality recognise, by "use-abolition", that the land has ended its role as a public object, then buy it by "sell-off (disposal by sale)". The rough order is: survey the current state and cadastral map → boundary (fude-kai) survey → apply for use-abolition → apply for the sell-off and pay the price → ownership-transfer registration.

| Stage | Content | Window / who |
|---|---|---|
| Use-abolition | For an extra-legal public object that has in fact lost its function, abolish its use as a public object | Municipality (by its extra-legal-public-object management ordinance/guideline) |
| Sell-off (disposal by sale) | After use-abolition, buy the land, now ordinary property, at market value | Municipality |
| Drafting/submitting documents | Drafting the use-abolition/sell-off application documents | Administrative scrivener |

The requirements for use-abolition are set by each municipality, but published examples list that it is clear the target section has in fact lost its function and is unused, that use-abolition will not make neighbouring land an enclosed (landlocked) parcel, and that there is no road-widening or development plan and no need to retain it. Some municipalities require the consent of neighbouring owners and the neighbourhood association. An akamichi/waterway that has not lost its function is, in principle, not a target for use-abolition/sell-off. Whether it is possible is judged by the municipality where the property is. ## Who does the boundary (fude-kai) survey and subdivision that the sell-off presupposes?

To take a sell-off, a survey confirming the boundary (fude-kai) with the neighbouring land, to identify the extent of the extra-legal public object bought, and a subdivision as needed, are the premise. This is a land and house investigator's professional field.

Here it helps to distinguish "fude-kai" (the registered boundary) from the "ownership boundary". The fude-kai is the line marking the registered extent of a parcel; parties cannot move it by agreement, and where it is unclear there is a fude-kai-identification system under the Real Property Registration Act (Act No. 123 of 2004) art. 123 and after. The ownership boundary is the extent of ownership decided by the parties' agreement or dispute; the two may not coincide. In a sell-off, the boundary is confirmed with the municipality and neighbouring owners present, and a survey plan is made to fix the extent.

| Work | Content | Who |
|---|---|---|
| Boundary (fude-kai) survey and attendance | Confirm the boundary with the municipality and neighbours, make a survey plan | Land and house investigator |
| Subdivision registration | Registration of description dividing the part to be sold off as one parcel | Land and house investigator |
| Land-category-change registration | Registration of description changing the land category to building land, etc. after the sell-off | Land and house investigator |
| Ownership-transfer registration on sell-off | Registration of rights transferring ownership from the municipality to the buyer | Judicial scrivener |

Because the extent of the sell-off cannot be set until the boundary is fixed, the survey is in many cases needed in parallel with use-abolition/sell-off. The general flow of a boundary survey is covered in [the boundary survey and sale of inherited real estate](https://luck428.com/en/column/souzoku-fudosan-kyoukai-kakutei-sokuryo-baikyaku). Subdivision and land-category change are registration of description by a land and house investigator; the ownership transfer on sell-off is registration of rights by a judicial scrivener—the roles differ. This article shows only the line of division of roles and leaves the individual survey/registration to the qualified professionals.

## Can I proceed while inheritance registration is unfinished? Who do I refer registration, tax and disputes to?

If the inherited land is still in the deceased's name, inheritance registration comes first. Inheritance registration became mandatory from 1 April 2024 (Real Property Registration Act art. 76-2), and failing to apply within the period without a justifiable reason can be subject to a non-penal fine. Without the name moved to the heir, the sell-off and sale are hard to proceed.

## Frequently asked questions

**Q. Can I sell inherited land even though an akamichi/waterway cuts into the site?**
A. The land itself is the heir's, so you can start preparing to sell. But the akamichi/waterway part is in many cases the municipality's and cannot be used as site, so in practice you use-abolish it, take a sell-off, fold it into the site and then sell. Whether use-abolition/sell-off is possible is judged by the municipality where the property is. This article shows only the line; the individual judgment rests with the municipality and qualified professionals.

**Q. Is the window for the sell-off the State, or the municipality?**
A. By the reform of the Act on Special Measures Concerning State Property following Act No. 87 of 1999, functioning extra-legal public objects were transferred for free from the State to municipalities by 31 March 2005. So a functioning akamichi/waterway is in many cases managed by that municipality, which is also the window for use-abolition and sell-off. Those that lost their function and were not transferred are managed by the Ministry of Finance (a Finance Bureau). Which it is, confirm with the municipality.

**Q. Is a survey always required before the sell-off? Who do I ask?**
A. To identify the extent bought, a boundary (fude-kai) survey with the neighbouring land is often the premise, and this is a land and house investigator's work. The fude-kai is a registered line the parties cannot move by agreement; where unclear there is the fude-kai-identification system under Real Property Registration Act art. 123 and after. Subdivision and land-category change are registration of description by a land and house investigator; the ownership transfer on sell-off is registration of rights by a judicial scrivener.

**Q. Can I proceed with use-abolition or the sale even if inheritance registration is not done?**
A. If the name is still the deceased's, the sell-off and sale are hard to proceed, and inheritance registration usually comes first. Inheritance registration became mandatory from 1 April 2024 (Real Property Registration Act art. 76-2), and failing to apply within the period without a justifiable reason can be subject to a non-penal fine. Inheritance registration is a judicial scrivener's field. We organise the order—moving the name to the heir first, then on to use-abolition, sell-off and sale.
