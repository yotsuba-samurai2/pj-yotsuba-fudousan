**The key point**: Even a delivery/takeout-only operation (a ghost kitchen) with no dine-in needs a restaurant-business permit if it cooks food served to customers (Food Sanitation Act (Act No. 233 of 1947) art. 55(1); Enforcement Order art. 35 item 1 "restaurant business"). In choosing a property, three points bite first: the use zone, drainage, and the health centre's facility standards. The use zone is set by the Building Standards Act (Act No. 201 of 1950) art. 48 and Appended Table 2: restaurants in principle cannot be built in a Category 1 low-rise exclusive residential zone; up to 150 m² of floor area in a Category 2 low-rise, 500 m² in a Category 1 mid/high-rise, 1,500 m² in a Category 2 mid/high-rise, 3,000 m² in a Category 1 residential zone; and they cannot be built in an exclusive industrial zone. For drainage, the Sewerage Act (Act No. 79 of 1958) art. 10 requires drainage equipment, and oily kitchen wastewater usually requires a grease trap (oil separator) under 1975 Ministry of Construction Notice No. 1597 and each municipality's sewerage ordinance/drainage standards. ## In which use zone can a delivery-only kitchen open?

Even delivery-only, so long as cooking takes place in the kitchen, it is generally treated as a "restaurant" under the Building Standards Act. Whether it is allowed and the size cap per use zone are set by art. 48 and Appended Table 2. The absence of dine-in does not remove this floor-area cap. Roughly as follows (an outline; floor-area counting, the building authority's ordinances and district plans may narrow it further).

| Use zone | Restaurant: allowed / size guide |
|---|---|
| Category 1 low-rise exclusive residential | In principle not allowed (only exceptions such as a combined-use house with a store part of 50 m² or less) |
| Category 2 low-rise exclusive residential | 2 storeys or fewer and floor area 150 m² or less |
| Category 1 mid/high-rise exclusive residential | 2 storeys or fewer and floor area 500 m² or less |
| Category 2 mid/high-rise exclusive residential | 2 storeys or fewer and floor area 1,500 m² or less |
| Category 1 residential | Floor area 3,000 m² or less |
| Category 2 residential / quasi-residential / neighbourhood commercial / commercial / quasi-industrial | Restaurants allowed (caps are large) |
| Industrial | Restaurants allowed |
| Exclusive industrial | Not allowed |

When looking for a small low-investment unit in a residential zone, this floor-area cap is the first gate. Note that if the operation leans towards large-scale cooking running several brands in one kitchen, it can in some cases be treated closer to a "factory" under the Building Standards Act, and the classification of use splits by property. Confirm which use it is, and whether a use-change application is needed, with an architect/building authority.

## Is a restaurant-business permit needed even without dine-in, and what does the health centre look at?

It is. An operation that cooks food served to customers is subject to a restaurant-business permit regardless of whether people eat on-site (Food Sanitation Act art. 55(1); "restaurant business" under Enforcement Order art. 35 item 1). With the 2018 amendment (fully in force 1 June 2021), the permit business types were reorganised from the 34 set by Cabinet Order to 32, HACCP-based hygiene control was made mandatory, and a notification system was newly created for operations not needing a permit (art. 57).

What the health centre looks at on the facility is the facility standards that Food Sanitation Act art. 54 delegates to ordinances. On top of the common standards in the Enforcement Regulations in force since 1 June 2021 sit the ordinances of prefectures and designated cities. Even for a delivery-only unit, points such as dividing the work area, a handwashing station, refrigeration/freezing equipment, a two-tub sink and hot water, and floors/walls that are easy to clean do not change. Even running several brands in one kitchen, a permit is in principle taken per facility. Whether the structure and equipment can meet the standards is safest consulted with the health centre before you secure the property. The classification of the permit and drafting the application are a certified administrative scrivener's; the individual conformity judgment of the facility standards is the health centre's.

## How much of the drainage, grease trap and water supply is provided on the property side?

Restaurant kitchen wastewater carries oils and residue, so discharging it as is causes pipe clogging and harm to the sewer. In a public-sewer service area, Sewerage Act art. 10 requires drainage equipment, and a grease trap (oil separator) that separates and catches oil is usually required under 1975 Ministry of Construction Notice No. 1597 (technical standards for drainage equipment) and each municipality's sewerage ordinance/drainage standards. Where wastewater may exceed the standards, an ordinance may order a pollutant-removal facility (Sewerage Act arts. 12, 12-11).

In a fitted-out (居抜き) unit, even if the previous tenant was a restaurant, you need to check before contracting whether the grease trap's capacity suits your operation, whether it has been inspected/cleaned, and whether the water supply/drainage/hot-water bore and exhaust duct are usable. Where there is no grease trap or its capacity is short, later installation/expansion is needed, and the underfloor pipe work bears on restoration and fixtures under the lease. See also [points to check on a skeleton/fitted-out restaurant unit](https://luck428.com/en/column/inshokuten-skeleton-bukken-haiki-grease), which handled oil and exhaust, and [property requirements for a kitchen-car prep site](https://luck428.com/en/column/kitchen-car-shikomi-basho-bukken-eigyo-kyoka-youken), where cooking is not completed in the vehicle—both are useful on how drainage and permits are thought about. The design and capacity judgment of piping and exhaust are an architect's/equipment contractor's field.

## How do fitted-out units, shared kitchens and whole-building leases differ in contract and restoration?

Delivery-only entry forms broadly divide into renting a unit in a shared kitchen (cloud kitchen) where the operator already holds a permit, and leasing a unit/whole building yourself and fitting it out from fitted-out or skeleton condition. The contract and restoration issues differ.

| Form | Points to check |
|---|---|
| Shared kitchen (use a unit by the hour or month) | Whether the restaurant-business permit is on the facility operator's side or can be taken separately for your unit / the permit holder and renewal / the scope of exclusive vs. shared use of the unit / how heavy restoration is on exit |
| Fitted-out lease (kitchen equipment remains) | Capacity and current state of water supply/drainage/grease trap/exhaust duct / whether fixtures are transferred and at what cost / whether restoration is skeleton-return or as-is / whether the lease purpose includes a restaurant business |
| Skeleton lease (no interior) | Whether water/gas/electricity capacity can be brought in / whether a new grease trap is possible / use-change application / initial investment and removal cost on exit |

If you want to start low-investment and short-term, a shared kitchen is easy to enter, but constraints can appear later over the permit holder and the handling of the exclusive unit. If you lease yourself, confirm before contracting whether the lease purpose includes a "restaurant business (delivery)" and whether subleasing or swapping brands is allowed. The final evaluation of whether contract clauses are favourable is a lawyer's field.

## What points in consideration for neighbours (smell, delivery-rider traffic, noise) bear on property choice?

A delivery-only operation, in exchange for no dine-in customers, tends to rub against neighbours through delivery riders' motorbike/bicycle traffic and waiting, kitchen-exhaust smells, and the sounds of prep and late-night operation. Checking the following at the property-choice stage makes post-opening trouble easier to avoid.

- Exhaust smell: the position and direction of the exhaust duct, distance to neighbours' windows/balconies. Odour can fall under the Offensive Odor Control Act or a municipality's living-environment ordinance
- Delivery-rider flow: whether a waiting/parking space for motorbikes/bicycles can be secured on-site, whether waiting on the frontage road obstructs passage
- Noise/time of day: cooking and vehicle sounds late at night/early morning. For a ground-floor unit in a condominium, the use/time limits of the management rules

Separately from whether a permit can be obtained, these can be factors that restrict the operation itself through the lease or management rules. We provide the property's location and the neighbourhood situation in a current-state survey before contracting.

## Frequently asked questions

**Q. Is a restaurant-business permit needed for a delivery-only operation with no dine-in?**
A. So long as food served to customers is cooked in the kitchen, a restaurant-business permit is needed regardless of dine-in (Food Sanitation Act art. 55(1); Enforcement Order art. 35 item 1). With the 2018 amendment (fully in force 1 June 2021) the permit types were reorganised from 34 to 32, and operations not needing a permit moved to a notification system (art. 57). Judging which classification applies and drafting the application are a certified administrative scrivener's field; the conformity judgment of facility standards is the health centre's.

**Q. Can I open a delivery-only kitchen even in a residential property?**
A. It depends on the use zone. Restaurants in principle cannot be built in a Category 1 low-rise exclusive residential zone, and have size caps such as 150 m² in Category 2 low-rise and 500 m² in Category 1 mid/high-rise (Building Standards Act art. 48, Appended Table 2). Delivery-only is generally treated as a restaurant under the Building Standards Act, and this floor-area cap is not removed just because there is no dine-in. Confirm the classification of use and whether a use-change is needed with an architect/building authority.

**Q. Can I use the grease trap as is in a fitted-out property?**
A. Not necessarily. You need to check whether the previous tenant's operation and capacity suit yours, and whether it has been inspected/cleaned. In a public-sewer area, installing drainage equipment is mandatory (Sewerage Act art. 10), and a grease trap is usually required for oily kitchen wastewater under a notice and the municipality's ordinance. If capacity is short or there is none, installation/expansion is needed, and pipe work bears on restoration and fixtures. The capacity judgment is an architect's/equipment contractor's field.

**Q. Can I run several delivery brands in one kitchen?**
A. It is done as an operating form, but a restaurant-business permit is in principle taken per facility. In a shared kitchen, check the permit holder on the operator's side and the handling of the exclusive unit; if you lease yourself, check whether the lease purpose includes swapping brands and subleasing—all before contracting. The permit classification is the health centre's/certified administrative scrivener's field, and the evaluation of contract clauses is a lawyer's.
