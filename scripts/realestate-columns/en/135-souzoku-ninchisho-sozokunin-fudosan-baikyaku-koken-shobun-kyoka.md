The estate-division agreement that is a precondition to selling does not validly form without the capacity to make a declaration of intent (Civil Code art. 3-2), and to take part in the agreement or sell on the person's behalf, an adult guardian chosen by the family court is needed. ## Why does a sale stall when an heir has dementia?

To sell inherited real estate, the usual flow is to settle the estate-division agreement that fixes whose property it is, do the inheritance registration, and then proceed to the sale. But an estate-division agreement is a legal act by all the heirs together, and each heir needs the capacity to make a declaration of intent (the ability to understand and judge matters).

Article 3-2 of the Civil Code provides that "if a party to a legal act did not have mental capacity at the time of the declaration of intent, that legal act is void" (newly established by Act No. 44 of 2017, in force 1 April 2020). An estate-division agreement joined by an heir who lacks the capacity to judge due to dementia has that person's declaration of intent voided, and the whole agreement fails to form.

| Stage | When there is an heir who lacks capacity |
| --- | --- |
| Estate-division agreement | The person's declaration of intent is void; the family cannot sign or act as agent on their behalf |
| Inheritance registration | Without the division settled, the title change toward a sale does not progress |
| Sale contract | If the person is a party, a contract made while lacking capacity is void |

So it is not that it "cannot be sold," but that the procedure to appoint someone who can lawfully judge and sign on the person's behalf (an adult guardian) is needed first.

## When an adult guardian is appointed, when and how can it be sold?

For a person who lacks capacity, the family court chooses an adult guardian. Article 7 of the Civil Code provides that the family court may, on the petition of the person, their spouse or a relative within the fourth degree etc., make a ruling to begin guardianship for a person who is in a constant state of lacking the capacity to discern right from wrong due to a mental disorder; Article 8 makes a person for whom guardianship has begun an adult ward, to whom an adult guardian is attached.

Once an adult guardian is chosen, they can join the estate-division agreement and become a party to registration and the sale on the person's behalf. The rough order is as follows.

| Order | Content | Who handles it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Petition to begin guardianship (to the family court) | The person, a relative, etc.; document support from a lawyer or judicial scrivener |
| 2 | The family court's ruling and appointment of the adult guardian | Family court |
| 3 | The adult guardian joins the estate-division agreement | Adult guardian (in a position to protect the person's interest) |
| 4 | Inheritance registration | Judicial scrivener |
| 5 | Sale contract and settlement (for a residence, the family court's permission is a precondition) | The seller (guardian) + brokerage is ours |

Note that the guardian is in a position to protect "the person's interest." They cannot agree to a division that sells cheaply for the relatives' convenience or reduces the person's share. If the guardian is also one of the co-heirs, their interests conflict, so a special agent must be appointed separately (Civil Code arts. 826 and 860).

## Why does selling a residence need the family court's permission?

Even with an adult guardian appointed, that alone does not let them freely sell real estate in the person's name. Article 859-3 of the Civil Code provides that for an adult guardian to dispose, on behalf of the adult ward, of a building used for their residence or its site—by sale, lease, termination of a lease, creation of a mortgage, or any other disposition equivalent to these—they must obtain the permission of the family court.

Because losing one's place to live greatly affects the person's life and mental state, from the viewpoint of protecting the person, the disposition of residential real estate specially requires the family court's permission. A sale of residential real estate made without permission is understood to be void (as in the National Tax Agency's written reply, among others).

| Character of the property | Family court permission (art. 859-3) |
| --- | --- |
| A building and its site the person now lives in, lived in before, or plans to live in | Required (a sale without permission is void) |
| Inherited real estate that is not the person's residence | Permission under art. 859-3 is not required; but the guardian's duty of care and reporting to the family court remain |

"Used for residence" is said to tend to be interpreted relatively broadly in practice, and judging whether it applies is the field of the family court and the guardian. We cannot assert "permission is / is not required." The thinking on sales involving residence-related rights such as a spousal right of residence is also useful in [Organising the sale of a building with a spousal right of residence (Japanese)](https://luck428.com/en/column/souzoku-haiguusha-kyojuuken-tatemono-baikyaku).

## Is there a way to sell before the estate division?

We also hear "we can't wait until guardianship begins" or "we want to sell before the division," but as long as there is an heir who lacks capacity, you cannot sell first by leaving the person out. The conceivable paths are limited.

| Path | Point to note |
| --- | --- |
| Sell after waiting for guardianship to begin | The standard flow. If it is the person's residence, the family court permission under art. 859-3 is also needed |
| Renounce inheritance to leave the group of heirs | A person who lacks capacity cannot do this alone; the guardian's involvement is needed. Not a choice to make lightly |
| Where there is a will | The person's participation in an agreement may become unnecessary; but the will's validity must be confirmed separately |

In every case, feasibility depends on the individual circumstances and legal judgment. There is information touting a "shortcut to selling before division," but a method that departs from protecting the person risks being voided later. However much you are in a hurry, not skipping the guardianship procedure is often the shortest route in the end. Confirm the need for and feasibility of procedures with a lawyer, judicial scrivener or the family court.

## Frequently asked questions

**Q. My parent has dementia. Can't we just discuss it as a family, sell the home, and sign on their behalf?**
A. An estate-division agreement or sale contract joined by an heir who lacks capacity does not form, because the person's declaration of intent is void (Civil Code art. 3-2). The family cannot sign or act as agent on their behalf; the procedure to appoint an adult guardian chosen by the family court is needed first. Confirm feasibility and steps with a lawyer or judicial scrivener.

**Q. Once an adult guardian is appointed, can we sell the home right away?**
A. The guardian can become a party to the agreement or sale on the person's behalf, but if the property to sell is the person's residence, separate family court permission is needed (Civil Code art. 859-3). A sale of residential property without permission is understood to be void. Judging whether it is a residence is the field of the family court and the guardian.

**Q. Can I (the child) become the guardian?**
A. A relative may sometimes become guardian, but the family court chooses. If the guardian is one of the co-heirs, their interests conflict in the estate division, so a special agent may need to be appointed separately (Civil Code arts. 826 and 860). Consult a lawyer, judicial scrivener or the family court on the appointment and conflict-of-interest judgment.

**Q. Does the guardianship procedure take time? Can we look for a buyer first?**
A. A ruling to begin guardianship and permission to dispose of residential property take a certain period. Because a contract made before the guardian is appointed or permission is granted risks being void, keeping the order is safer. We can advise, in parallel with the procedure, on organising market conditions and preparing the sale, and proceed to contract and settlement once the requirements are met.
