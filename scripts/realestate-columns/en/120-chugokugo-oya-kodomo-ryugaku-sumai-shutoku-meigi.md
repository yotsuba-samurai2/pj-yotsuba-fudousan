## For a child's study or work, how does the home differ between the parent's name and the child's?

The first thing to separate is "who becomes the buyer (owner)." Whether the parent (usually a non-resident) or the child living in Japan, the Foreign Exchange Act report, the source of funds and gift tax, and the taxation on a future sale all change. Which is better is not decided uniformly; judge it by the source of funds, the expected length of stay and the policy for future disposal.

Ownership of real estate in Japan is not restricted by nationality or residence status. Residence status concerns the activities one can carry out in Japan (study, work, etc.) and is a separate matter from whether one can own real estate. Buying in the name of a child who is studying, or in the name of a parent living outside Japan, is therefore possible for ownership itself. The dividing line is in the procedures and the taxes.

| Name | The buyer's standing | Mainly what comes into play |
|---|---|---|
| Parent's name (living abroad) | Non-resident | Foreign Exchange Act acquisition report / settlement and remittance / the buyer's withholding when sold later |
| Child's name (living in Japan) | Resident (during their stay) | If the parent funds it, the gift-tax (deemed gift) issue / the relationship to the child's stay |
| Joint (parent and child) | A resident and a non-resident mixed | The above coexist share by share, and procedures tend to double |

Under any name, the property search and sale brokerage are ours; tax estimates go to a tax accountant and registration to a judicial scrivener.

## When the parent buys while living abroad (non-resident), how do settlement, remittance and tax move?

If the parent buys while living abroad (as a non-resident), a report under the Foreign Exchange Act is required after acquisition. Because the remittance and settlement cross borders, identity verification and the scheduling of the receipt of funds are needed, and when that property is later sold while still owned by a non-resident, a withholding obligation arises on the buyer's side. The reporting and tax procedures are assigned to someone other than the property search.

Under the Foreign Exchange and Foreign Trade Act, where a non-resident acquires real estate in Japan or rights relating thereto, a "report on the acquisition of real estate in Japan or rights relating thereto" must be submitted to the Minister of Finance via the Bank of Japan within 20 days of acquisition (FEFTA Art. 55-3(1)(12) and related provisions). The size of the amount or area does not matter. By the ministerial-ordinance amendment in force on 1 April 2026, the acquisition of real estate itself — including acquisition for residential use — was added to the reporting target "regardless of purpose." The filing may be made by the acquirer themselves or by an agent who is a resident (such as the real estate agent). The details, the form and the content of the 2026 amendment are summarised in [acquisition of real estate by non-residents and the Foreign Exchange Act report](https://luck428.com/en/column/hikyojusha-fudosan-shutoku-gaitameho-houkoku). Estimates of non-resident taxation, deemed transfer, and withholding on a future sale go to a tax accountant.

| Situation | What moves | Who |
|---|---|---|
| After acquisition | Foreign Exchange Act acquisition report (within 20 days, via the Bank of Japan) | The person, or a resident agent (we can be involved as broker) |
| Settlement and remittance | Identity verification for overseas remittance; schedule for the receipt of funds | Financial institution / the person |
| During ownership | Receipt of fixed-asset tax notices, etc. (consider a tax agent) | Tax accountant confirms |
| Future sale | A purchase from a non-resident carries withholding on the buyer's side | Tax accountant estimates |

## How do you align the child's residence status ("Student," "Engineer/Specialist") with acquiring or renting the home?

The child's residence status defines "what they do in Japan" and is not directly tied to whether they can own or rent a home. However, the outlook for their stay (whether the status is renewed, the move to work after graduation) is a factor in which name to buy in and when to sell or let in future. The eligibility for, or change of, the residence status itself is handled, separately from the property, by an administrative scrivener (immigration filing agent).

The residence statuses "Student" and "Engineer/Specialist in Humanities/International Services" are categories of activity set out in Appended Table I of the Immigration Act (Immigration Control and Refugee Recognition Act; Cabinet Order No. 319 of 1951). Whether one falls within them is judged by the Immigration Services Agency. Acquiring or renting a home is not itself a requirement of the residence status, but the expiry date of the period of stay and the timing of graduation or the move to work affect the design of whether to keep, let or sell. For a lessor's points when letting after the child returns home or moves, see [what a landlord should check when letting to a foreign tenant](https://luck428.com/en/column/gaikokujin-nyukyo-oya-no-fuan); for keeping and managing it from abroad, see [managing Japanese real estate while living overseas (Chinese support)](https://luck428.com/en/column/kaigai-kyoju-fudosan-kanri-chinese). ## When the child eventually returns home or moves, what are the options to sell or let?

Thinking about the "exit" at the buying stage keeps your options from narrowing later. When the child graduates, returns home or moves, the home can be organised into "sell," "let" or "keep." If the parent sells while still a non-resident, withholding on the buyer's side is involved; if they let it, a receiving base in Japan (management, tax agent) is needed.

When a non-resident sells while still the owner, the buyer in principle withholds 10.21% of the consideration for the property (10% income tax plus 0.21% special reconstruction income tax) (with certain exceptions, such as where the buyer is an individual acquiring for their own or a relative's residential use and the consideration is ¥100 million or less; the detail is confirmed by a tax accountant). When letting, there are situations where 20.42% is withheld from the rent if the tenant is a corporation or the like, and you arrange the owner's receiving base abroad (notification of a tax agent, a management company). When keeping it, the management of a vacancy and the receipt of fixed-asset tax, etc., are needed. For any exit, the sale/letting practice is ours, tax estimates go to a tax accountant, and registration to a judicial scrivener.

## Frequently asked questions

**Q. Can I buy a home in the name of a parent (living in China or Taiwan) for a child studying in Japan?**
A. You can. Real estate in Japan can be owned regardless of nationality or residence status. However, where the parent is a non-resident, a Foreign Exchange Act report (report on the acquisition of real estate in Japan, via the Bank of Japan) is required within 20 days of acquisition. The report can also be filed by an agent who is a resident (such as the real estate agent), and we can be involved as broker. Tax estimates go to a tax accountant and registration to a judicial scrivener.

**Q. If we buy in the child's name but the parent pays, what about tax?**
A. If the parent bears the price and puts it in the child's name, it may be treated as a gift under the Inheritance Tax Act (including deemed gift). Because the taxation relationship also changes with the resident/non-resident distinction, it is safest to decide the name together with a tax estimate. Whether tax actually applies and the amount vary by case, so this article does not assert them (unverified). Consult a tax accountant before the contract.

**Q. Does the child's residence status ("Student," "Engineer/Specialist") give any advantage in owning or renting a home?**
A. Residence status is a category of the activity carried out in Japan, not a requirement for owning or renting real estate. Ownership is possible even without a residence status. That said, the expiry date of the stay and the outlook for graduation or the move to work are factors in whether to keep, sell or let. Eligibility for, or change of, residence status is for an administrative scrivener (immigration filing agent); the property search is ours.

**Q. Is there any special procedure when, in future, the child returns home and that home is sold?**
A. If the owner sells while still a non-resident, the buyer withholds in principle 10.21% of the consideration (with exceptions, such as an individual buyer for their own or a relative's residential use with a consideration of ¥100 million or less). When letting, there are situations where 20.42% is withheld from the rent if the tenant is a corporation or the like. For either, tax estimates go to a tax accountant, the sale/letting practice is ours, and registration to a judicial scrivener.
