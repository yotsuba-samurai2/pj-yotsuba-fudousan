**In short:** A leasehold (shakuchiken) is the right to rent another person's land and own a building on it. Unlike freehold ownership, rent, a contract term and the landowner's consent follow it. When buying, first confirm the remaining term, the rent, whether consent and a consent fee are needed, and whether a home loan can be arranged.

## What is a leasehold, and how does it differ from freehold ownership?

A leasehold is the right to rent another person's land for the purpose of owning a building on it (a superficies right or a land lease). The land belongs to the landowner; the leaseholder pays rent to use the land and owns only the building on it under their own name. That the land and the building have separate owners is the greatest difference from freehold ownership (where both land and building are your own).

The Land and Building Lease Act (Act No. 90 of 1991) defines a leasehold as "a superficies right or a land lease for the purpose of owning a building." In Japan, if you register the building on the leased land under your own name, you can assert the land right even against third parties, so leaseholder protection is strong. For clients in mainland China and Taiwan, explaining it as close to a "land-use right" (the land belongs to the state or another, the building is yours) often helps, while the handling of renewal and term differs by system and must be checked against the Japanese statute.

| Point | Freehold ownership | Leasehold |
|---|---|---|
| Right to the land | Yours | The landowner's (rented) |
| Right to the building | Yours | Yours |
| Ongoing cost | None (only fixed-asset tax) | Rent (a renewal fee / consent fee may also arise) |
| Term | None | There is a contract term (ordinary leaseholds renewable; fixed-term leaseholds end at expiry) |
| Home loan | Easy to arrange | Can be harder (the lender may require the landowner's consent) |

The Act came into force on 1 August 1992. Leaseholds set before that are in principle still governed by the old Leasehold Act (Act No. 49 of 1921), with the post-renewal term and the like handled differently from the current law. The older the leasehold, the more you must first confirm which law applies (the final judgment on applicable law is made by a lawyer).

## How does the exit differ between an ordinary and a fixed-term leasehold?

An ordinary leasehold can be used almost indefinitely because it can be renewed, whereas a fixed-term leasehold has no renewal and the land is returned to the landowner at expiry. Because the exit (what happens in the future) is the exact opposite, you must always tell them apart before buying.

The term of an ordinary leasehold is 30 years, and the contract may set a longer period (Art. 3). The post-renewal term is 20 years for the first renewal and 10 years thereafter (Art. 4). Even when the term expires, if the leaseholder requests renewal and there is a building on the land, it is deemed renewed on the same terms unless the landowner objects without delay on justifiable grounds (Art. 5). A special clause disadvantageous to the leaseholder that contravenes these is void (Art. 9).

A fixed-term leasehold is a leasehold set for a term of 50 years or more under which no renewal and no building-buyback request are provided; this special clause must be made in writing, such as by notarial deed (Art. 22). For those whose purpose is owning a building solely for business use, there are fixed-term leaseholds for business of 30 years or more but under 50 years, or 10 years or more but under 30 years, set by notarial deed (Art. 23). A fixed-term leasehold ends at expiry and the land is in principle returned as vacant land, so the shorter the remaining term, the fewer the years the building can be used.

| Type | Provision | Term | Renewal | At expiry |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ordinary leasehold | Arts. 3, 4, 5 | 30 years or more (post-renewal 20 → 10 years) | Yes (statutory renewal exists) | Continues if conditions met |
| Fixed-term leasehold | Art. 22 | 50 years or more | None | Returned in principle as vacant land |
| Fixed-term leasehold for business | Art. 23 | 10 years or more, under 50 years | None | Returned in principle as vacant land |

## When buying a leasehold-land property, what should you check? (remaining term, rent, consent fee, loan)

The first things that matter are four: (1) the remaining term, (2) the amount of rent and whether it is revised, (3) whether the landowner's consent and a consent fee are required, and (4) whether a home loan is available. Unlike a freehold property, getting these wrong leads straight to exit problems such as "few usable years after purchase" or "cannot resell because the landowner's consent cannot be obtained."

The remaining term can be extended by renewal for an ordinary leasehold, but for a fixed-term leasehold it ends at expiry, so the years to expiry directly affect value. Rent is a monthly ongoing cost; check whether the contract has a revision clause. For a loan, where a building on leased land is used as collateral, lenders often require the landowner's consent (consent to transfer and to the creation of security), so the options are narrower than for a freehold property. When a non-resident in the Chinese-speaking world is the buyer, reporting under the Foreign Exchange and Foreign Trade Act and tax confirmation also overlap (a related point in [reporting and remittance for overseas owners](https://luck428.com/en/column/hikyojusha-yachin-gensen-2042)).

| Item to check | Point to watch | Whom to consult |
|---|---|---|
| Remaining term | Ordinary or fixed-term; years to expiry | Us (property survey) / lawyer (applicable-law judgment) |
| Rent | Monthly amount, revision clause, arrears | Us / landowner |
| Consent and consent fee | Whether consent is needed for transfer, extension/rebuild | Us (negotiation within brokerage) / lawyer (court = leasehold non-contentious case) |
| Home loan | Whether the lender finances leaseholds; whether a consent letter is needed | Us / lender |

## In which situations is the landowner's consent required? What is the rough consent fee?

The landowner's consent is generally required when you sell (transfer to a third party) a leasehold, when you extend, rebuild or renovate the building, or when you sublet. It depends on the contract, but inheriting and succeeding to it as-is does not itself require consent.

To transfer or sublet a leasehold (land lease) to a third party, the landowner's consent is in principle required. Where the landowner does not consent, the leaseholder may petition the court for "permission in lieu of the landowner's consent," and where the landowner withholds consent despite there being no risk of disadvantage to them, the court may grant permission in lieu of consent (Art. 19; a so-called leasehold non-contentious case). This petition and procedure are a lawyer's area; what we do is limited to negotiation with the landowner within the scope of brokerage.

There is **no statutory standard for consent fees** (name-change fees, rebuild-consent fees, renewal fees, etc.). They are settled by agreement with the landowner and by individual circumstances, and the figures cited as customary vary widely. The following are only in the "it is sometimes said" range; the actual amount is fixed by negotiation with the landowner and, if a dispute arises, by a lawyer's judgment (all figures are **unverified**).

| Name of consent fee | Figure often cited (no legal basis; unverified) | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Transfer-consent fee (name-change fee) | Said to be roughly 10% of the leasehold price | Paid to the landowner on sale |
| Rebuild/renovation-consent fee | Said to be a few percent of the vacant-land price | When rebuilding the building |
| Renewal fee | By contract and local custom | There may be no legal obligation to pay |

Avoid asserting as if there were a legal going rate for consent fees. Telling Chinese-speaking clients that "a consent fee is not a fixed rate like a tax, but a cost decided by negotiation with the landowner" helps prevent misunderstanding.

## How are leaseholds treated on sale or inheritance?

A leasehold can be bought and sold as property and is succeeded to on inheritance. Inheriting and succeeding to a leasehold as-is does not require the landowner's consent, but selling it to a third party in principle does. For inheritance-tax valuation, a monetary value is recognised in the leasehold too.

For inheritance and gift tax, the value of a leasehold is found by multiplying the land's value as self-used (vacant) land by the "leasehold ratio." The leasehold ratio is set by area and shown on the roadside-land-price map and the valuation-multiplier table (National Tax Agency Tax Answer No. 4611, "Valuation of leaseholds"; accessed 12 October 2026). Fixed-term leaseholds and the like are in principle valued on the basis of the economic benefit the leaseholder receives and its term. The specific valuation and tax are a tax accountant's area, which we do not do. The estimate of capital-gains tax on a sale gain is likewise a tax accountant's.

When selling a building on leased land together with the leasehold, the landowner's consent and the transfer registration of the building and leasehold are required. ## Frequently asked questions

**Q. I hear a leasehold is cheaper than freehold but harder to sell. Why?**
A. Because the land is not yours, and rent, a contract term and the landowner's consent follow it. In particular, selling to a third party in principle requires the landowner's consent (Art. 19), and a home loan can be harder to arrange. The price is lower than freehold, but confirming the exit is more important.

**Q. What happens to a fixed-term leasehold property when the term ends?**
A. A fixed-term leasehold has no renewal; it ends at the expiry of the term, and the building is in principle demolished and the land returned to the landowner as vacant land (Arts. 22, 23). The remaining years to expiry are the usable period, so always confirm the remaining term before buying.

**Q. If the landowner will not consent to a sale of the leasehold, can it not be sold?**
A. Where the landowner withholds consent despite no risk of disadvantage, you may petition the court for permission in lieu of consent (Art. 19, a leasehold non-contentious case). This petition is a lawyer's area. What we can do is negotiation within the scope of brokerage; the court procedure is separated to a lawyer under a separate contract.

**Q. What is the market rate for a consent fee?**
A. There is no statutory standard for consent fees; they are decided by agreement with the landowner and by individual circumstances. It is sometimes said to be "about 10% of the leasehold price," but that is only a customary guide, varies widely, and is unverified. It is not a fixed rate, so do not assert it — confirm it through negotiation with the landowner.
