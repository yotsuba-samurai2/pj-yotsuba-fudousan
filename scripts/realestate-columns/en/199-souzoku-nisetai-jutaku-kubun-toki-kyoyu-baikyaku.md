A two-household home built with a parent and child household sharing the cost changes greatly, in both how it is sold and how it is taxed, by "how it is registered." Even if it looks like one building, in the register it may be one building (co-ownership or sole ownership) or two separate buildings—a parent part and a child part (partitioned registration). Proceed with a sale not knowing this difference and the relief you expected may not apply, or the sale may stall because one registered owner's consent cannot be obtained. ## How does "partitioned registration" versus "co-ownership" change the way you sell?

Even the same two-household home takes one of roughly three registration forms. First, "sole registration"—in the sole name of the parent or child, with one building. Second, "co-ownership registration"—one building held in shares by parent and child. Third, "partitioned (condominium-type) registration"—the parent part and child part registered as separate buildings (partitioned units) under Articles 1 and 2 of the Act on Building Unit Ownership, as structurally and functionally independent exclusive sections. Even a fully separated type with separate entrances and no internal passage is not necessarily registered as partitioned. You cannot tell without looking at the register (the certificate of all recorded matters).

The effect on selling is as follows. With sole or co-ownership the building is one, so it is easier to arrange a sale of the whole building and lot together. With partitioned registration, the parent part and child part are each independent ownership, so the sellers and what is sold are split. The land is, in principle for condominium ownership, treated together with the exclusive section as a right to use the site, but two-household homes often hold the land in co-ownership, so the rights in the land and the building must be checked one by one.

| Registration form | Number of buildings | Basic way to sell |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sole registration | 1 | The owner can sell the whole building and lot together more easily |
| Co-ownership registration | 1 | Sell the whole building with all co-owners' consent (a share alone is also theoretically sellable) |
| Partitioned (condominium) registration | 2 | Each owner of the parent/child part sells their own part; the land rights also need checking |

## With partitioned registration left as is, who sells what?

A partitioned-registration two-household home has the parent part and child part as separate partitioned buildings. In inheritance, the parent part that stood in the parent's name becomes inheritance property, while the child part in the child's name is the child's, unrelated to the inheritance. In other words, what the heirs can sell is, in principle, only the parent part and the land rights matching it. Even if there is a buyer who wants the whole building (parent part plus child part) as one, it cannot be sold as a single building unless the child part's owner (the child) also intends to sell.

From the buyer's view, buying only one part is limited in use and tends to fetch a lower price. In practice, the parent and child (the heir and the child-part owner) align, and the two units and the land rights are arranged to be sold together to the same buyer at the same time—a "simultaneous sale"—so it can be treated as equivalent to one whole building. This arrangement—aligning the timing of contracts, the important-matters explanation, checking the site-use right—is real estate (our) practice. The inheritance (ownership) registration that underlies who holds which unit or share is a judicial scrivener's, and the registration of description such as partition and merger a land and house surveyor's.

| Scene | Who can sell | What can be sold |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Only the parent part is inherited | The heir of the parent part | The parent-part unit + matching land rights |
| Wanting to sell the whole building together | Heir of the parent part + owner of the child part | The two units + land, sold simultaneously |
| Wanting to sell only the child part | Owner of the child part | The child-part unit (possible alone, but buyers are limited) |

## Does the small-scale residential land relief become unusable with partitioned registration?

Japan’s inheritance-tax relief for small-scale land may reduce the assessed value of qualifying residential land by 80% for up to 330 square metres (Act on Special Measures Concerning Taxation, Art. 69-4). The starting points are the use of the plot just before the inheritance began, whether the building was registered as separate units, and who lived where at that time. Separate entrances or the absence of an internal passage do not settle these questions.

For the category covering a decedent’s residential land acquired by a relative who lived with them, separate unit registration means the occupancy test looks at the part occupied by the decedent. Without separate unit registration, the decedent's and relatives' dwelling areas are considered, so the land serving the relative’s part may also qualify even if the entrances are separate. Other categories may apply, including land used by a relative who shared the decedent’s livelihood. The requirements differ for spouses and co-resident relatives. For instance, the co-resident relative category requires continued residence and ownership up to the inheritance-tax filing deadline. Ask a Japanese tax accountant before selling or moving out.

| State of the two-household home | Living-together test (inheritances on/after 1 Jan 2014) | Reach of the relief (general) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| No separately registered units (shared or sole ownership) | Areas occupied by the decedent or relatives are considered | Land serving the relative’s dwelling may also qualify if the acquirer meets the relevant requirements |
| Separately registered units | The occupancy test considers the part occupied by the decedent | For the decedent's residential land category, generally the land serving that unit; check other categories separately |

## Should you do a merger registration, and when and who does it?

If the statutory conditions are met, adjacent units can be merged into one building on the register under Article 54(1)(iii) of the Real Property Registration Act. This can simplify the building rights presented to a buyer. A merger recorded after the death does not change which units were registered, or who lived where, immediately before that death for the inheritance under consideration.

Article 56 limits merger registration where the registered owners differ, where co-ownership shares differ, and in certain cases involving mortgages and other registered rights. Whether an exception applies depends on the register. If you are considering reorganising the ownership before an inheritance, ask a tax accountant and a judicial scrivener about any needed transfers and their tax effects. Land and house surveyors handle merger and other descriptive registrations; judicial scriveners handle rights registrations. We do not file them on your behalf.

| Judgment factor | Merits of a merger registration | Points to note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ease of sale | Rights become simple as one building; easier to attract a buyer | A merger has structural and functional requirements |
| Small-scale land relief | A merger after the death does not change the facts for the initial inheritance assessment | Ask a tax accountant about the position before the death and any requirements until the filing deadline |
| Rights and cost | Future procedures are simplified | A mortgage may make a merger impossible (Reg. Act Art. 56) |

## When the parent and child shares differ, how is the sale proceeds split? (separate engagement)

A two-household home is an asset prone to dispute over how to split the sale proceeds, because the parent and child often differ in how the construction cost was borne and in the registered shares. Inheritance property is co-owned by the heirs until the estate division is complete (Civil Code Art. 898), and the division is made considering the kind of property, each heir's age, occupation and circumstances of life, and all other circumstances (Civil Code Art. 906). With partitioned registration the market value of each unit, and with co-ownership the share ratio, is the starting point for allocation, but how to reflect the record of construction-cost burden and the state of use is for the heirs' consultation and, failing agreement, the field of a lawyer and the family court. The fixed amounts, tax and procedural costs vary greatly with the property and the family makeup, so this article does not assert them and leaves them as unverified.

## Frequently asked questions

**Q. Where can I tell whether a two-household home is partitioned-registered?**
A. Check with the building's certificate of recorded matters (all recorded matters) obtained at the Legal Affairs Bureau. If registered as a partitioned building, the parent part and child part are recorded as separate buildings (exclusive sections). Even a fully separated type with separate entrances and no internal passage is not necessarily partitioned-registered. It cannot be judged by appearance, so we organise the register and the land rights before sale.

**Q. With partitioned registration, is the small-scale residential land relief unusable?**
A. Separate unit registration does not rule out the relief altogether. The applicable land and acquirer requirements depend on the registration, occupancy and land use immediately before the inheritance began. For example, co-resident relatives must continue to live there and keep ownership until the filing deadline. Ask a Japanese tax accountant before selling.

**Q. Should I do a merger registration into one building before selling?**
A. It may simplify the building rights for a sale, but a merger after death does not change the registration and occupancy immediately before the inheritance. Owners, shares and mortgages can limit whether a merger is possible. Ask a land and house surveyor about the merger, a judicial scrivener about inheritance registration and a Japanese tax accountant about relief and when to sell.

**Q. When the parent and child bore different construction costs, how is the sale proceeds split?**
A. With partitioned registration the market value of each unit, and with co-ownership the registered share ratio, is the starting point. Inheritance property is co-owned until division (Civil Code Art. 898), and the division is made considering all circumstances (Art. 906). How to reflect the record of the construction-cost burden is for the heirs' consultation. Failing agreement, it is the field of a lawyer and the family court; we organise the property's market-value information and the sale arrangement.
