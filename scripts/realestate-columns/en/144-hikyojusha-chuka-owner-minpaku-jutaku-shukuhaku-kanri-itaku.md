## How do a minpaku (residential lodging business), a special-zone minpaku, and a hotel business differ?

A business that lodges people for a fee broadly falls into three frameworks—residential lodging business (minpaku), special-zone minpaku, and hotel business—each with its own governing law and procedure. Which framework you use changes both the conditions required of the property and where you file.

Residential Lodging Business Act art. 2 (3) defines a residential lodging business as "a business, by a person other than an operator under Hotel Business Act art. 3-2 (1), of lodging people in a dwelling for a fee, where the days people are lodged do not exceed 180 in a year." So a minpaku is a scheme using a dwelling, within a cap of 180 days offered a year. A hotel business (Hotel Business Act) has no day cap; a special-zone minpaku, as a special exception to the Hotel Business Act under National Strategic Special Zones Act art. 13, is a business of letting people use a facility for at least a set minimum number of nights within a certified zone.

| Framework | Governing law | Procedure | Day guide |
|---|---|---|---|
| Residential lodging business (minpaku) | Residential Lodging Business Act art. 3 | Notification to the prefectural governor (or a city/ward with a health centre) | Days offered ≤ 180 a year (art. 2 (3)) |
| Special-zone minpaku | National Strategic Special Zones Act art. 13 | Certification by the prefectural governor etc. (special certification); only in certified zones | The use period is set by cabinet order at or above a period fixed by ordinance within a range of 2 nights/3 days to 9 nights/10 days |
| Hotel business (simple lodging etc.) | Hotel Business Act art. 3 | Licence from the prefectural governor etc. | No day cap |

A special-zone minpaku can only be used in a certified zone (e.g. Ota Ward in Tokyo, Osaka City) and is a separate procedure in which the municipality's certification replaces the Hotel Business Act art. 3 (1) licence. Which framework a property's location can use varies with zoning, the zone and the management rules. The final judgment on which framework to file under is the municipality's jurisdiction.

## In the "host-absent" type, where the host is not in Japan, who do you entrust with what?

The Residential Lodging Business Act requires, for a form in which the owner is absent while guests stay, that the management work for the notified dwelling be entrusted to a registered residential-lodging manager. A non-resident owner, in principle, needs this entrustment.

Art. 11 (1) provides that where either (i) the number of guest rooms in the notified dwelling exceeds the number of rooms set by order of the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism and the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (five rooms under the enforcement regulation), or (ii) the operator is absent (other than a temporary absence set by order) while guests are lodged in the notified dwelling, the operator "must entrust the residential-lodging-management work for that notified dwelling to one residential-lodging manager." Item (ii) is the category that practice calls the "host-absent" type. The terms "host-present" and "host-absent" are not statutory terms but a classification in the ministerial order and the Japan Tourism Agency's guidelines; the basis for the entrustment duty is in the items of art. 11 (1).

A temporary absence means a short outing such as buying daily necessities; the Tourism Agency's guidelines treat it as, in principle, around one hour (at most two hours given traffic conditions). A non-resident owner who does not live in Japan does not fall within this temporary absence, so is usually treated as host-absent and needs to entrust the management work.

The manager entrusted must, under art. 22 (1), be a business registered with the Minister of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism, and the registration is valid for five years (art. 22 (2)). Once entrusted, the measures in arts. 5 to 10—ensuring sanitation and safety, guidance in a foreign language, keeping a guest register, explanation on preventing adverse effects on the living environment of the surrounding area, and handling complaints—are carried out by the manager (art. 11 (2)).

## Where do you check whether the condominium management rules prohibit a minpaku?

Whether a unit in a condominium can be a minpaku is checked, ahead of the law, by confirming that the building's management rules and use bylaws do not prohibit a residential lodging business. If the rules prohibit it, a minpaku is not possible even if the statutory requirements are met.

The national model management rules were amended in 2017 so that each management association can decide in its rules whether to "permit" or "prohibit" a residential lodging business. In practice many associations put in rules that prohibit it, and permitting a minpaku requires a provision allowing it. The notification under art. 3 also calls for confirming that the management rules do not prohibit it. The starting points for confirmation are as follows.

| What to confirm | Where to look |
|---|---|
| The minpaku clause in the management rules / use bylaws | The latest rules held by the management association/company; obtain them from the seller |
| General-meeting minutes (any resolution prohibiting/permitting a minpaku) | The association's general-meeting minutes |
| An inquiry to the management association | If the rules are silent, confirm with the association whether a minpaku is allowed |
| The lessor's consent for a leased property | For a sublease type where the operator differs from the owner, the lease and the lessor's consent |

## Frequently asked questions

**Q. Can I turn a property into a minpaku even though I don't live in Japan?**
A. Turning it into a minpaku (a residential lodging business) is itself possible. But under Residential Lodging Business Act art. 11 (1), for the host-absent type in which the owner is absent while guests stay, the management work for the notified dwelling must be entrusted to a residential-lodging manager registered with the Minister of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism. A non-resident owner who does not live in Japan does not fall within a temporary absence and is usually treated as host-absent, so the entrustment is needed. The notification goes to the prefectural governor (or a city/ward with a health centre), and the annual days offered are capped at 180.

**Q. What counts as "host-absent"?**
A. The text (art. 11 (1) (ii)) excludes a temporary absence from "being absent while guests stay." A temporary absence means a short outing such as buying daily necessities; the Tourism Agency's guidelines treat it as, in principle, around one hour (at most two hours given traffic conditions). An owner living abroad exceeds this range and is usually treated as host-absent. The final judgment on this falls under the municipality's jurisdiction.

**Q. Can I run a minpaku even in a condominium unit?**
A. The premise is that the management rules and use bylaws do not prohibit a residential lodging business. The national model management rules were amended in 2017 so that the association can decide in its rules whether to permit or prohibit a minpaku, and many associations have rules that prohibit it. The notification also calls for confirming that the rules do not prohibit it. Obtaining the latest rules, use bylaws and general-meeting minutes from the association/management company and checking them is the starting point.

**Q. Who do I consult about tax on minpaku income?**
A. Where a non-resident earns rental/lodging income from real estate in Japan, issues of withholding, tax filing and appointing a tax agent arise. This is a tax accountant's work; neither we nor an administrative scrivener or a manager can judge or file in their place. This article is general information and does not state a particular tax position, so confirm amounts and whether filing is required with a tax accountant.
