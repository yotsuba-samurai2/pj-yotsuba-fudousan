## Where do the risks lie for the buyer in a retaining wall or cliff?

The risks of a retaining wall or cliff divide broadly into "the physical risk of collapse" and "the risk to rebuilding and asset value." What the buyer should grasp first is the latter — the presence of legal regulation and of certificates of inspection. Art. 19(4) of the Building Standards Act (Act No. 201 of 1950) requires that, where a building may suffer damage from a cliff collapse and the like, a retaining wall be installed or other appropriate safety measures be taken. In other words, if the existing wall does not meet the standards, redoing the wall may be required when rebuilding.

Whether the existing wall was built lawfully can be judged at first hand from the presence of certificates of confirmation and inspection, because a wall over 2 metres high is subject to the confirmation and inspection of a structure (see below). A wall without certificates tends to create a burden in proving safety or in its treatment on rebuilding, and may affect price and financing (the degree is individual; we do not assert it, unverified). Judging the safety of the structure itself is the field of an architect or civil-engineering specialist.

| Type of risk | Buyer's check | Whom to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Physical collapse | Structure and ground of the wall | Architect / ground survey firm |
| Rebuilding constraints | Cliff ordinance; whether the wall must be redone | Specified administrative agency / architect |
| Asset value / financing | Presence of inspection certificate; regulated area | Real estate appraiser / lender |

## How do you find out whether it is within a regulated area under the Embankment Regulation Act?

The former Act on Regulation of Residential Land Development was reorganised by Act No. 55 of 2022 into the "Act on Regulation of Residential Land Development, Specified Embankments, etc." (commonly, the "Embankment Regulation Act"), and the amending act came into force on 26 May 2023. Prompted by the 2021 debris-flow disaster in Atami City, it is a framework regulating dangerous embankments and the like under nationally uniform standards regardless of land use (jointly administered by the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism and the Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries).

Under this Act, the prefectural governor and the like designate two kinds of regulated area: the "residential-land-development-work regulated area" (Art. 10), such as around urban areas and settlements, and the "specified-embankment regulated area" (Art. 26), which carries danger from topographical conditions even away from urban areas. To carry out certain embankment or excavation work within a regulated area, the prefectural governor's permission is required before starting the work (Art. 12 for the former area; Art. 30 for the latter). Because designation progresses differently by municipality, check on the published maps of the prefecture and municipality where the property is (designation status varies by municipality; unverified).

| What to check | Basis | How to find out |
|---|---|---|
| Residential-land-development-work regulated area | Embankment Regulation Act Art. 10 | Published maps of the prefecture / municipality |
| Specified-embankment regulated area | Embankment Regulation Act Art. 26 | Same |
| Whether work in the area is permitted | Embankment Regulation Act Arts. 12, 30 | Municipal permission records; history of development |

## What is the problem if the retaining wall has no certificate of inspection or confirmation?

A retaining wall over 2 metres high is subject to building confirmation and completion inspection as a structure, under Art. 88(1) of the Building Standards Act and Art. 138(1)(v) of the Enforcement Order of that Act (Cabinet Order No. 338 of 1950). A lawfully built wall retains certificates of confirmation and inspection, and these are first-hand evidence that it "was built in line with the standards." Art. 142 of the Enforcement Order requires, as technical standards for the wall, non-corroding materials such as reinforced concrete or masonry, the provision of weep holes, and confirmation of safety by structural calculation.

For a wall without certificates of confirmation or inspection, proving legality is hard, and on rebuilding you may be required to redo the existing wall rather than use it, or additional structural study may be needed. This can affect construction cost, schedule and asset value, though the degree is individual and we do not assert it (unverified). In the buyer's DD, check the wall's height, the presence of certificates of confirmation and inspection, and the history of development (when and by whom), leaving the evaluation of the structure's safety itself to an architect or civil-engineering specialist.

| What to check | Basis | In charge |
|---|---|---|
| Whether the wall is subject to confirmation and inspection | Building Standards Act Art. 88(1); Enforcement Order Art. 138(1)(v) (over 2 m) | Specified administrative agency |
| Conformity with technical standards | Enforcement Order Art. 142 | Architect |
| Presence of certificates | Certificates of confirmation and inspection | Specified administrative agency / ledger |

## How does the cliff ordinance bite on rebuilding and reconstruction?

Cliff ordinances are set by each prefecture and city in a building safety ordinance and the like under the Building Standards Act, and their content differs by municipality. In Tokyo's example, Art. 6(2) of the Tokyo Building Safety Ordinance provides that where a building is built, or a building site developed, within a horizontal distance of twice the cliff height from the lower end of a cliff over 2 metres high, a retaining wall over 2 metres high must be installed. However, this is excluded where there is no safety problem, such as where the slope gradient is 30 degrees or less, or where a slope is cut into firm ground.

Further, Art. 6-2 of the same ordinance provides that the bottom of the wall's foundation must reach good ground within a 30-degree gradient passing the lower end of the cliff. These govern whether a new or redone retaining wall becomes necessary when rebuilding an existing building on cliff-side property. Even on land where "a house now stands," the wall conditions may bite at reconstruction and generate additional cost, so confirm the cliff ordinance of the property's location with the specified administrative agency at the DD stage. Because the figures and application of the ordinance differ by municipality, a per-property inquiry is a precondition.

| What to check | Basis (Tokyo example) | Where to confirm |
|---|---|---|
| Whether it falls in the regulated range | Tokyo Building Safety Ordinance Art. 6(2) (within twice the cliff height) | Specified administrative agency |
| Whether a new/redone wall is needed | Art. 6 (wall over 2 m) | Specified administrative agency / architect |
| Ground under the wall foundation | Art. 6-2 (good ground within a 30-degree gradient) | Architect / ground survey firm |

## Frequently asked questions

**Q. Where can I find out whether it is within a regulated area under the Embankment Regulation Act?**
A. Check on the regulated-area maps published by the prefecture and municipality where the property is. Whether it is a residential-land-development-work regulated area (Art. 10) or a specified-embankment regulated area (Art. 26) changes whether work in the area needs permission (Arts. 12, 30). Because designation progresses differently by municipality, inquire with the municipality per property.

**Q. If the retaining wall has no inspection certificate, must I not buy?**
A. You are not immediately barred from buying. But a wall over 2 metres high is subject to confirmation and inspection (Building Standards Act Art. 88(1); Enforcement Order Art. 138(1)(v)), and without certificates a burden may arise in proving legality or in redoing the wall on rebuilding. Ask an architect or civil-engineering specialist to evaluate the structure's safety, and confirm the effect on price and financing with a real estate appraiser and the lender.

**Q. A house stands there now, so why does the wall become an issue at reconstruction?**
A. Because Art. 19(4) of the Building Standards Act and cliff ordinances require safety measures for the wall at the time of building (including rebuilding). In the Tokyo Building Safety Ordinance Art. 6 example, a wall is required where you build within twice the cliff height from the lower end of a cliff over 2 metres high. Even with an existing building, the wall conditions may bite on reconstruction, so confirm with the specified administrative agency in DD.

**Q. Will the broker judge the wall's safety as well?**
A. No. What brokerage and the important-matters explanation can handle is limited to restrictions under laws — whether it is in a regulated area, the presence of inspection certificates, and the application of the cliff ordinance (Real Estate Brokerage Act Art. 35(1)(ii)). Judging whether the wall is structurally safe is the field of an architect or civil-engineering specialist, to whom we connect you as an independent entity engaged separately. No referral fees are exchanged.
