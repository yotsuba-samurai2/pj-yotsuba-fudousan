**The key point**: When you inherit a rental house or apartment with a tenant still in residence, whether to vacate it and sell (obtain surrender) or to sell with the tenant (owner change) completely changes the exit depending on whether it is an ordinary lease or a fixed-term lease. For an ordinary lease, a refusal to renew or a notice of termination by the lessor cannot be made unless just cause is recognised (Land and Building Lease Act art. 28). Just cause is judged comprehensively from: the circumstances in which the lessor and the lessee (including a sublessee) each need to use the building; the past course of the lease; the building's state of use and present condition; and the property benefit (eviction money) that the lessor offers as a condition of, or in exchange for, surrender. An ordinary lease with a fixed term is deemed renewed by operation of law if the renewal-refusal notice is not given between one year and six months before expiry (art. 26); where there is no fixed term, it ends six months after the lessor's notice of termination (art. 27) — but in both cases just cause is the premise. By contrast, a fixed-term lease (Land and Building Lease Act art. 38), if the requirements are met, has no renewal and ends on expiry, so the exit design differs. ## How does an inherited rental house differ between selling after vacating and selling with the tenant?

When you inherit a rental house with a tenant, the heir succeeds, as it is, to the position of the lessor. There are broadly two ways to sell: having the tenant vacate so you sell after it is empty (bare land or vacant house), or selling with the tenant still in residence together with the lessor position — an "owner change (with-tenant)." Which you choose changes the required procedure, time and the way to think about net proceeds.

When selling after vacating, an ordinary lease needs just cause and, in many cases, eviction money, and it takes time. On the other hand, when selling with the tenant, you can sell without going through surrender, but because the buyer evaluates it on yield (income), the way of thinking about price may differ from selling to a buyer for own use. Which suits you changes with the tenant's attributes, the contract contents and whether the heir wants to cash out quickly.

| Exit | What is needed | Suited situation |
|---|---|---|
| Sell after vacating | For an ordinary lease, just cause + eviction money, and time for agreement or surrender | Want to sell to own-use buyers / sell after clearing the land |
| Sell with tenant (owner change) | No surrender needed; sell succeeding to the lease as is | Want to sell quickly / sell to investors as an income property |

## What does the 'just cause' needed for surrender of an ordinary lease refer to?

To sell after vacating, you need the tenant to leave. But under an ordinary lease, the lessor cannot unilaterally end the contract on the lessor's own convenience alone. The key here is "just cause" under Article 28 of the Land and Building Lease Act.

Article 28 of the Land and Building Lease Act provides that a lessor's refusal to renew (the notice under art. 26(1)) or notice of termination of a building lease cannot be made unless just cause is recognised. And just cause is judged comprehensively from the following elements. An ordinary lease with a fixed term is deemed renewed on the same terms as before (but with no fixed term) if the parties do not give the renewal-refusal notice between one year and six months before expiry (art. 26(1)). An ordinary lease with no fixed term ends six months after the lessor's notice of termination (art. 27(1)). In either case, a termination initiated by the lessor has just cause under Article 28 as its premise.

| Element of just cause (Land and Building Lease Act art. 28) | Content |
|---|---|
| Need to use | The circumstances in which the lessor and the lessee (incl. a sublessee) each need to use the building |
| Past course | The past course of the building lease |
| State of use | The building's state of use |
| Present condition | The building's present condition |
| Property benefit | The property benefit (eviction money) offered as a condition of, or in exchange for, surrender |

What is important here is that the lessor-side circumstance of "because I want to sell" alone is not necessarily sufficient as just cause. If the tenant's need to use the building is high, it is judged comprehensively together with the lessor's need and the eviction money. The legal evaluation of whether just cause exists is a lawyer's field. We do not conclude "this property will be recognised to have just cause"; we help, as general information, with the framework of the statutes and the traffic control of when to consult a lawyer.

## How is eviction money positioned, and what forms the basis of a sense of its market rate?

Eviction money always comes up in talk of surrender. Eviction money is the "property benefit" that Article 28 of the Land and Building Lease Act lists as an element of just cause, and is positioned as supplementing the lessor's just cause. In other words, it is not of a nature that paying eviction money necessarily secures surrender; whether just cause exists is judged comprehensively together with the other elements (need to use, past course, state of use, present condition).

There is no formula fixed by law or a uniform market rate for the amount of eviction money. In general, the costs of the tenant's relocation (moving costs, the rent difference of a new property, etc.), the interest the tenant loses, and the circumstances of both lessor and lessee are taken as materials, and ultimately it depends on the parties' agreement, or on the court's judgment in litigation if agreement cannot be reached. The level of a specific amount changes greatly by location, use (residential or business), rent and the course of the contract, and the trend of case law is not uniform.

| Issue around eviction money | Content |
|---|---|
| Legal positioning | A "property benefit" that supplements just cause (Land and Building Lease Act art. 28) |
| Nature | Eviction money alone does not fix surrender; judged comprehensively with other elements |
| Material for the amount | Relocation costs, rent difference, lost interest, both parties' circumstances, etc. (no uniform rate) |
| How it is decided | The parties' agreement, or the court's judgment in litigation if none |

## How do the exits differ between a fixed-term lease and an ordinary lease?

Even for the same "rental house," the exit differs entirely depending on whether the contract is a fixed-term lease or an ordinary lease. When you check the contract of an inherited rental house, this is the first fork.

An ordinary lease, as seen so far, requires just cause (Land and Building Lease Act art. 28) for termination initiated by the lessor and is a system premised on renewal. By contrast, a fixed-term lease (the fixed-term building lease of art. 38 of the Land and Building Lease Act) is a type that ends on expiry of the term set in the contract, without being renewed. To validly form a fixed-term lease, there are requirements such as a contract by a document (including an electromagnetic record) like a notarial deed, and giving a document in advance to explain that there is no renewal and it ends on expiry. With a fixed-term lease that meets the requirements, surrender is easier to obtain toward expiry (going through the notices etc. that the law requires), and just cause and eviction money do not come to the fore as much as in an ordinary lease.

| Item | Ordinary lease | Fixed-term lease (Land and Building Lease Act art. 38) |
|---|---|---|
| Renewal | In principle yes (renewal by law) | None (ends on expiry) |
| Termination by lessor | Just cause needed (art. 28) | Ends on expiry if requirements met |
| Eviction money | Tends to become an issue | Tends not to come to the fore |
| Formation requirements | Can form even orally | Requirements of a document, prior explanation, etc. |

Note that whether an inherited contract is a fixed-term or an ordinary lease is decided by the contract's wording and whether the formation requirements are met. Even if intended as a fixed-term lease, if it lacks the requirements it may be treated as an ordinary lease, and that judgment is a lawyer's field. ## Frequently asked questions

**Q. Can I have the tenant leave my inherited rental house just because I want to sell?**
A. For an ordinary lease, a refusal to renew or a notice of termination by the lessor cannot be made unless just cause is recognised (Land and Building Lease Act art. 28). Just cause is judged comprehensively from the circumstances in which the lessor and the tenant need to use the building, the past course, the building's state of use and present condition, and the offer of a property benefit such as eviction money. "Because I want to sell" alone is not necessarily sufficient; it is weighed together with the tenant's need to use. The legal evaluation of whether just cause exists is a lawyer's field, and we do not conclude it.

**Q. If I pay eviction money, will I definitely get surrender? How much is the market rate?**
A. No. Eviction money is a "property benefit" that supplements just cause (Land and Building Lease Act art. 28); eviction money alone does not fix surrender. Whether just cause exists is judged comprehensively together with the need to use, the past course, the state of use and the present condition. There is no uniform rate or formula for the amount of eviction money; with relocation costs, the rent difference and both parties' circumstances as materials, it is decided by the parties' agreement, or by the court's judgment in litigation if none. Estimating the amount and negotiation are a lawyer's field, and we do not conclude an amount.

**Q. Can I sell with the tenant still in residence, without vacating?**
A. Yes. It is a sale by "owner change (with-tenant)," selling together with the lessor position while the tenant still lives there. You can sell quickly because you do not go through surrender, but because the buyer evaluates on income (yield), the way of thinking about price may differ from selling empty to an own-use buyer. Whether selling vacated or with the tenant suits you changes with the tenant's attributes, the contract contents and whether you want to cash out quickly.
