You inherit a condominium from a parent and, when you go to sell, find the management fees and repair reserves had long gone unpaid—this is not unusual. The heir inherits both the rights and the obligations of the decedent (Civil Code Art. 896), so the arrears too are in principle inherited. The tricky part is that this arrears is not "over once you sell"—it follows the person who buys. ## Why does the buyer owe even the previous owner's (the decedent's) unpaid management fees?

Article 7(1) of the Act on Building Unit Ownership grants the manager or the management-association corporation a statutory lien over claims held against other unit owners regarding common areas, the building site and the like, and claims based on the bylaws or a general-meeting resolution. Management fees and repair reserves are debts each unit owner bears under the bylaws and a general-meeting resolution, so they fall within these claims. On that basis, Article 8 provides that "the claims specified in paragraph 1 of the preceding Article may also be exercised against the specific successor of the unit owner who is the debtor." A buyer who acquires the unit ownership by sale is a "specific successor" and can be billed by the management association for the previous owner's unpaid management fees and repair reserves. Because the text does not make the buyer's good or bad faith (whether they knew of the arrears) a requirement, even a buyer who did not know can be subject to the succession.

Meanwhile, because the heir inherits all the rights and obligations of the decedent as a universal succession (Civil Code Art. 896), the arrears the decedent owed are inherited by the heir. If the heir sells, that arrears also reaches the buyer (the specific successor)—a double structure. Note that, by the Supreme Court judgment of 23 April 2004, claims for management fees and the like are organised as periodic-payment claims subject to a short (5-year) extinctive prescription (now 5 years under Civil Code Art. 166), so the treatment of the time-barred portion of the arrears turns on whether the prescription has been renewed by acknowledgment or a judicial claim. What portion is time-barred is a legal judgment, and the feasibility of collection and the negotiation are a lawyer's field. This article does not assert any individual prescription outcome and leaves it as unverified.

| Position | Basis | Relation to the unpaid fees |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Heir | Civil Code Art. 896 (universal succession) | Inherits the decedent's arrears |
| Buyer (specific successor) | Act on Building Unit Ownership Art. 8, Art. 7(1) | Can be billed by the association for the previous owner's arrears |
| Management association | Act on Building Unit Ownership Art. 7(1) (statutory lien) | Holds a statutory lien over the unit ownership and the like |

## How do you grasp the arrears accurately (what do you ask the management association for)?

The first thing before selling is to pin the arrears down as a figure. The heir asks the management association, or the entrusted management company, to disclose the balance (arrears) of management fees and repair reserves, the total of accumulated repair reserves, the amount of management fees, the long-term repair plan and repair history, the bylaws and use rules. In practice, the "investigation report on important matters (report on matters concerning management)" issued by the management company is used to check these together. Where late charges (delay damages) are set in the bylaws, you also check how much late charge has accumulated on top of the principal arrears.

This is an investigation that real estate (we) carries out in preparing the sale. Without accurately pinning down the fact and amount of arrears, it cannot be reflected in the price or in the important-matters explanation. Fixing the amount and the collection negotiation itself, and a dispute with the management association, are a lawyer's field; we handle up to grasping the facts and arranging the sale.

| What to request / check | What it reveals |
| --- | --- |
| Balance (balance certificate) of management fees and repair reserves | The amount that can pass to the buyer |
| Total accumulated repair reserves / amount of management fees | A mandatory item of the important-matters explanation |
| Presence and amount of late charges (delay damages) | A burden beyond the principal |
| Bylaws, use rules, long-term repair plan, repair history | The buyer's decision material and future burden |

## Which is better—clearing the arrears before selling, or selling at a discount with the arrears included?

There are broadly two ways to sell. First, the heir clears the arrears before handing over. The arrears do not pass to the buyer, so price negotiation is easier and the deal is more likely to close, but the heir must temporarily front the arrears. Second, sell with the arrears included, deducting that amount from the sale price. The heir need not put up their own funds, but the buyer lowers the price by the arrears (and late charges) they will succeed to. Which is more advantageous net varies with the arrears, late charges, the property's market and the buyer's profile, and cannot be said uniformly.

On tax, whether the heir can treat the management fees they paid as a selling expense, and how to use the added-acquisition-cost special measure, come into play, but these are a tax accountant's judgment. We organise the view of "how the net differs in the cleared case versus the arrears-included case," referring tax to a tax accountant and collection or negotiation to a lawyer.

## How far must you disclose the arrears in the important-matters explanation?

In the sale of a unit-ownership building, Article 35(1)(vi) of the Real Estate Transaction Business Act and Article 16-2 of its enforcement regulation provide that the bylaw provision on repair reserves and the amount already accumulated (item 6), and the amount of ordinary management fees (item 7), and the like must be explained as important matters. Further, the Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism's "Interpretation and Operation of the Real Estate Transaction Business Act" states, for item 6, that "where there are arrears of repair reserves and the like regarding the unit-ownership building, their amount is to be told," and for item 7, that "where there are arrears of management fees, their amount is to be told." In other words, the previous owner's arrears is among the matters to be explained as important decision material for the buyer.

The same interpretation and operation also requires, because these amounts vary over time, "recording as far as possible the most recent figure (such as the amount at the most recent settlement period) with the point in time stated." Sell while hiding the arrears and the buyer, as a specific successor, bears an unexpected burden—a possible later dispute. We accurately reflect the arrears grasped in the investigation into the important-matters explanation document. The scope and method of disclosure differ by transaction, and the final legal evaluation is a lawyer's field, so this article remains general information.

## If you renounce the inheritance, what happens to the obligation to pay the unpaid fees? (separate engagement)

If you renounce the inheritance, under Civil Code Art. 939 you are "deemed never to have been an heir as to that inheritance," so you do not owe the obligation to pay the decedent's debts, including the unpaid management fees. The renunciation is filed with the family court in principle within three months from when you learned of the commencement of the inheritance for yourself (Civil Code Art. 915(1)). However, renunciation has ripple effects. When a prior-rank heir renounces, the right of inheritance moves to the next-rank person, who faces the same decision. Also, if you are actually occupying the condominium at the time of renunciation, a duty remains to preserve it with the same care as your own property until you hand it over to the heir or the estate liquidator (Civil Code Art. 940). If all heirs renounce, ultimately a procedure such as appointing an estate liquidator is needed.

Whether to renounce, whether it is in time, and how to handle the management and liquidation after renunciation are legal judgments; the renunciation procedure and the application for an estate liquidator are a lawyer's and judicial scrivener's field, and the inheritance registration a judicial scrivener's. ## Frequently asked questions

**Q. Can I escape the payment obligation by selling without paying the unpaid fees?**
A. The arrears does not vanish even if the heir sells. The buyer, as a specific successor under Article 8 of the Act on Building Unit Ownership, can be billed by the management association for the previous owner's arrears, so selling while hiding the arrears becomes trouble with the buyer. The important-matters explanation under the Real Estate Transaction Business Act requires telling the amount of any arrears of repair reserves and management fees, and we accurately reflect the arrears grasped into the explanation document.

**Q. Where do I inquire to learn the amount of arrears?**
A. Ask the management association or the entrusted management company to disclose the balance (arrears) of management fees and repair reserves, the accumulated total, the presence of late charges, the bylaws and repair history. In practice, the management company's "investigation report on important matters" is used to check these together. This is an investigation we carry out in preparing the sale. Fixing the amount and the collection negotiation itself are a lawyer's field.

**Q. Which is more advantageous—clearing the arrears before selling, or selling with the arrears included?**
A. Not uniformly. Clearing before selling means it does not pass to the buyer and the deal is easier to close, but the heir must front it first. Including the arrears needs no fronting, but the buyer lowers the price by the arrears and late charges. The net comparison depends on the property's market and the amount, and the tax treatment is a tax accountant's judgment. We organise the view of the net in both cases.

**Q. If I renounce the inheritance, do I not have to pay the unpaid management fees?**
A. Renouncing means you are deemed never to have been an heir (Civil Code Art. 939), so you do not owe the arrears. The renunciation is filed with the family court in principle within three months (Civil Code Art. 915(1)). But there are ripple effects: the right moves to the next-rank heir, and if you were occupying it at renunciation a preservation duty remains until handover (Civil Code Art. 940). Consult a lawyer or judicial scrivener on whether and how to renounce.
