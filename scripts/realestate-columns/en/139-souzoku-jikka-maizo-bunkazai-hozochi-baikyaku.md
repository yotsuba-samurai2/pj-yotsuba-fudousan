**The key point**: Even if an inherited family home is in a "known buried-cultural-property site," you can sell the land itself. What the Cultural Properties Protection Act requires is a notification 60 days before starting civil-engineering work within the site (art. 93)—not a ban on the sale that transfers ownership. What affects price and saleability is that a buyer who builds or grades there must notify and may be required to undergo an excavation survey, and that the schedule and cost move depending on the survey. ## Where can you find out whether it is a "known buried-cultural-property site"?

First, inquire with the board of education of the municipality (city/ward/town/village) with jurisdiction. Many municipalities publish an archaeological-sites map and register on the web or at a counter.

A "known buried-cultural-property site" is land, premised by Cultural Properties Protection Act (Act No. 214 of 1950) art. 93, that is known to contain buried cultural property such as shell mounds and burial mounds. Which extent is known as a site is organised by each municipality (board of education) as an archaeological-sites map and register.

| How to check | Content |
|---|---|
| Municipal sites map / register | Published by the municipal cultural-property section (board of education); viewable on a web map or at a counter |
| Inquiry to the cultural-property section | Inquire about applicability showing the location/lot number; many municipalities issue a written reply |
| Adjacent / near the boundary | Even outside the mapped extent, if adjacent, a notification or attendance may be required |
| Condition on site | Also check whether there is a history of past grading or excavated finds |

Even within the same city, the lots that fall within a site and those that do not are split. For land about which we receive a sale inquiry, we check the likelihood of applicability from the municipality's sites map and inquiry to the cultural-property section, and connect it to the survey toward the important-matters explanation. The final judgment on applicability rests with the relevant board of education (we confirm based on public information as of 24 October 2026).

## When and who makes the notification for work on the land (Cultural Properties Protection Act art. 93)?

A person doing civil-engineering work within a site notifies 60 days before the planned start. The sale that transfers ownership itself does not require a notification.

Cultural Properties Protection Act art. 93 (1) requires a person who intends to excavate land for civil-engineering work or other purposes other than a survey of buried cultural property, within a known buried-cultural-property site, to notify the Commissioner for Cultural Affairs 60 days before the day the work is to start (in practice via the prefectural/municipal board of education). On receiving the notification, where necessary for the protection of buried cultural property, an instruction such as a change in the excavation method may be given (art. 93 (2)).

| Party | Procedure | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| General work-doer (heir, buyer, operator, etc.) | Notify 60 days before start | Cultural Properties Protection Act art. 93 (1) |
| National agency, local government, etc. | Prior notice in place of notification | Cultural Properties Protection Act art. 94 |
| Person excavating for (academic) survey | Excavation notification (30 days before) | Cultural Properties Protection Act art. 92 |

From the real-estate-transaction angle, the point to grasp is that **the notification duty attaches "when work is done," not to inheriting or selling the land itself**. Just selling it as cleared land or in its current state does not trigger a notification. A notification actually becomes necessary when the heir or buyer starts construction or grading. So for an heir considering a sale, the key is less "can it be sold" than whether you can correctly explain "what happens when the buyer builds." If notification documents become necessary, preparing them is the work of an administrative scrivener; we do not act as agent.

## Who bears the cost and the schedule for a trial survey and a full survey?

After the notification, a trial/confirmation survey and a full excavation survey are carried out as needed. The full survey's cost is in principle borne by the cause-party (the developer), but there is practice under which it may be publicly funded or state-subsidised for things like building a private home.

On receiving the notification or notice, from the need to protect buried cultural property, a trial/confirmation survey first checks whether there are remains/artefacts on the land, and if preservation is needed, a full excavation survey for record preservation may be instructed. The rough division of cost and schedule is as follows (it differs by municipal practice).

| Phase | Cost / schedule guide | Who handles it |
|---|---|---|
| Trial / confirmation survey | Often carried out by the municipality (board of education) | The relevant board of education |
| Full excavation survey (record preservation) | In principle borne by the cause-party (developer); can be several million yen or more and months in scale | The cause-party; but building a private, non-profit home, etc. may be publicly funded or state-subsidised |
| Effect on schedule | The start is pushed back by the survey period | Reflected in the construction plan |

How large the cost and schedule are varies greatly with the presence and extent of remains/artefacts, so consulting the municipality in advance before starting to confirm the outlook is the right order. The final judgment on whether and how much the cost is borne rests with the relevant municipality. ## How far must a buried-cultural-property site be explained in a sale (important-matters explanation)?

Being within a site directly affects the buyer's use of the land (construction, grading), so it is practice to explain it to the buyer in the important-matters explanation.

When a real-estate broker brokers a sale, it explains matters that materially affect the transaction decision to the buyer in the important-matters explanation under Real Estate Brokerage Act (Act No. 176 of 1952) art. 35. Being within a known buried-cultural-property site is a fact that a buyer, on construction or grading, will need the art. 93 notification and an excavation survey; because it ties directly to schedule, cost and funding plans, it is usually disclosed in the important-matters explanation.

| What is explained | Point |
|---|---|
| Applicability as a site | Whether and to what extent it applies per the sites map / board-of-education inquiry |
| Procedure at the time of work | That the art. 93 60-day-prior notification is needed for construction/grading |
| Possibility of a survey | That a trial/full survey may be instructed; effect on schedule/cost |

Note that there are differing views on whether a known buried-cultural-property site is a "restriction under laws and regulations" expressly listed in art. 3 of the Real Estate Brokerage Act Enforcement Order; this article does not assert either way (if needed, check art. 3 of the Enforcement Order directly on e-Gov Law Search). Regardless of express listing, it is practice to explain it as a matter that materially affects the buyer's decision. Neglecting the explanation risks later claims of non-conformity or breach of the explanation duty. We reflect the site-check result accurately in the important-matters explanation and explain, including the possibility that the buyer needs the notification and survey for construction or grading. Whether an individual notification/survey is needed is the board of education's judgment.

## Frequently asked questions

**Q. If my inherited family home is a known buried-cultural-property site, can it no longer be sold?**
A. It can be sold. What the Cultural Properties Protection Act requires is a notification 60 days before starting civil-engineering work within the site (art. 93 (1)), not a ban on the sale of the land itself. Just selling it as cleared land or in current state does not trigger a notification. What affects price and saleability is that a buyer who builds or grades must notify and may face an excavation survey, and that the schedule and cost move depending on the survey. We survey applicability and reflect it in the important-matters explanation.

**Q. Where can I find out whether it is a site?**
A. Inquire with the cultural-property section (board of education) of the municipality with jurisdiction. Many municipalities publish a sites map/register on the web or at a counter, and you can inquire about applicability showing the location/lot number. Near the boundary of the mapped extent, if adjacent, a notification or attendance may be required. For land about which we receive a sale inquiry, we also check the likelihood of applicability from the municipality's sites map and inquiry. The final judgment rests with the relevant board of education.

**Q. Who bears the cost of the excavation survey, and how much is it?**
A. A trial/confirmation survey is often carried out by the municipality (board of education), and the cost of a full excavation survey for record preservation is in principle borne by the cause-party (the developer). It can be several million yen or more and months in scale. However, building a private, non-profit home, etc. may be publicly funded or state-subsidised in practice. Because the cost/schedule varies greatly with the presence and extent of remains, consult the relevant municipality in advance before starting. The final judgment on who bears it rests with the municipality.

**Q. Does a buried-cultural-property site reduce the inheritance-tax valuation?**
A. There may be a valuation treatment such as deducting the equivalent of the excavation-survey cost, but whether it applies depends on the individual circumstances and the current treatment. Judging whether and how much a reduction applies is a tax accountant's field; we cannot assert it.
