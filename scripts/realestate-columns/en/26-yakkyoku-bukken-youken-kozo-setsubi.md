**In short:** for a dispensing pharmacy, two things bite first — the use zone (a pharmacy is treated as a "shop" and, unlike a clinic, is subject to use-zone restrictions), and the Structure and Equipment Regulations for Pharmacies, which require a floor area of roughly 19.8 m² or more, a dispensing room of 6.6 m² or more, and a clean, clearly separated partition. For a medical mall or a clinic-attached unit, you also look at prescription intake and the flow of movement. The final decision rests with the health centre and the specified administrative authority. Confirm the use zone and the makings of the partition before you sign, and you avoid the rework.

You lease a place thinking "the clinic is right next door, so of course a pharmacy fits," and then, at the licensing stage, you are told to redo the dispensing-room area or the partition. The same rework that hits restaurants and clinics hits dispensing pharmacies. This article is for pharmacists and healthcare operators opening or relocating a dispensing pharmacy in Tokyo, and for property owners planning a medical mall: what you can confirm before signing the lease, set out in order from the Act on Securing Quality, Efficacy and Safety of Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices (the "PMD Act") and the Structure and Equipment Regulations for Pharmacies. Final confirmation rests with the health centre and the specified administrative authority.

## What use zone lets you open a dispensing pharmacy?

Here is the biggest difference from a clinic. A clinic can be built in every use zone under the Building Standards Act's use restrictions, but **a pharmacy is treated as a "shop (a shop selling goods)" and is subject to use-zone restrictions.**

The Building Standards Act (Act No. 201 of 1950), in Table 2 and Article 48, sets out which buildings may be built in each use zone. A pharmacy is hard to build as a standalone shop in some residential zones; in a Category-1 low-rise exclusive residential zone it can, as a rule, only be built as a combined-use dwelling (at least half of the total floor area residential, shop portion 50 m² or less). In neighbourhood-commercial, commercial and quasi-industrial zones it is easier to build.

| Use zone | Rough treatment of a pharmacy (shop) |
|---|---|
| Category-1 low-rise exclusive residential | No standalone shop; only within a combined-use dwelling (residence ≥ ½ of total floor area, shop ≤ 50 m²) |
| Category-2 low-rise exclusive residential | May be built as an order-prescribed shop of a certain scale |
| Category-1/2 medium-to-high-rise exclusive residential | Area restriction eases; easier to build |
| Residential / quasi-residential / neighbourhood-commercial / commercial / quasi-industrial | May be built |

**"A clinic stands on the land, so a pharmacy can surely go in too" does not follow.** A clinic is free of the use restriction, but even on the adjoining lot or in the same building, a pharmacy is subject to use-zone restrictions as a shop. And when converting an office or a shop of another trade into a pharmacy, a use-change confirmation application may be required depending on the floor area. **The use-zone feasibility and whether a use-change confirmation is needed can be confirmed with the specified administrative authority before signing.** The order for a clinic property is at [what to confirm before signing for a clinic property](https://luck428.com/column/clinic-bukken-youto-chiiki-kaisetsu-todokede); how the use zone and floor-area ratio move pricing, at [why Japanese land prices change with the floor-area ratio](https://luck428.com/column/yosekiritsu-hosei-tochine).

## What must the structure-and-equipment standard (area, dispensing room, clean room) meet?

To open a pharmacy, Article 4, paragraph 1 of the PMD Act (Act No. 145 of 1960) requires the permission of the governor of the prefecture where it is located (or the mayor of a city / head of a special ward that runs a health centre). This permission must be renewed every six years (paragraph 4). Article 5 provides that permission may be withheld where, among other things, **the structure and equipment do not meet the standard set by ministerial ordinance.**

That "standard set by ministerial ordinance" is Article 1 of the Structure and Equipment Regulations for Pharmacies (Ministry of Health and Welfare Ordinance No. 2 of 1 February 1961). The items that bear directly on choosing premises are these (**accessed 28 August 2026**).

| Item | Structure-and-equipment standard for a pharmacy (extract) |
|---|---|
| Item 1 | Easy for those buying or receiving to enter, and clearly a pharmacy from its exterior |
| Item 2 | Sufficient ventilation, and clean |
| Item 3 | **Clearly separated** from other pharmacies/shops, from living quarters, and from unclean areas |
| Item 4 | Floor area **roughly 19.8 m² or more**, allowing the pharmacy's work to be done properly |
| Item 5 | Daylight/lighting giving illuminance (60 lux or more for display of drugs, 120 lux or more at the dispensing counter) |
| Item 7 | Equipment for **cold, dark storage** |
| Item 8 | A **lockable storage facility** |
| Item 10 | A **dispensing room of 6.6 m² or more**, with ceiling and floor of boarding, concrete or the like, and measures preventing purchasers from entering |

So on the property side, the starting point is **whether you can secure roughly 19.8 m² or more and, within it, build a dispensing room of 6.6 m² or more, clearly separated from living quarters and unclean areas.** The "clean room" often asked about is the **aseptic dispensing room** used where aseptic preparation (mixing high-calorie infusions and the like) is done for home medical care. That is a separate standard required only of pharmacies doing aseptic preparation — it needs equipment keeping the air cleanliness at ISO 14644-1 Class 7 or better at all times. It is unnecessary for ordinary dispensing, but if you plan home care, allowing room for it at the property stage avoids rework.

## What to watch in a medical mall or a clinic-attached unit?

A medical mall has a ready flow of patients and is easier to open in, but it has its own points as a property.

| Point | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Separation of structure | Item 3 requires "clear separation from other pharmacies/shops, living quarters and unclean areas"; confirm the partition between common areas and the dispensing room/waiting area on the drawings |
| Securing the area | Cutting the partition reduces effective floor area; does it fall below 19.8 m² / a 6.6 m² dispensing room? |
| Independence | Under the PMD Act, a pharmacy must be managerially and functionally independent of any particular medical institution; the making of the flow and signage is sometimes questioned |
| Rent vs. prescription outlook | The mix of stock changes with the attached clinic's specialty and prescribing pattern; balance against the rent |
| Use zone / use change | The mall's overall use, and the use-zone feasibility / need for a use-change confirmation of the pharmacy portion |

The **independence of the pharmacy from the medical institution** in particular touches the making of the property (entrances, flow, partitions), so it is safer to confirm the health centre's approach at the drawing stage before signing. ## Frequently asked questions

**Q. If a clinic can be built on the land, can a pharmacy open there too?**
A. Not necessarily. A clinic can be built in every use zone under the Building Standards Act's use restrictions, but a pharmacy is treated as a "shop" and is subject to use-zone restrictions. In a Category-1 low-rise exclusive residential zone it is, as a rule, limited to a combined-use dwelling. Feasibility varies by property, so confirm at the specified administrative authority's counter.

**Q. Is the floor area for a dispensing pharmacy fixed by law?**
A. Article 1 of the Structure and Equipment Regulations for Pharmacies sets the pharmacy's area at "roughly 19.8 m² or more" and the dispensing room at "6.6 m² or more." This is a nationwide ordinance standard. However, the operation of illuminance and equipment details, and the judgment of the necessary size, vary by locality and property, so confirm with the health centre with jurisdiction.

**Q. We plan to serve home medical care. What should we check at the property stage?**
A. If you do aseptic preparation (mixing high-calorie infusions and the like), you need an aseptic dispensing room. That is a separate standard required only of pharmacies doing aseptic preparation, needing equipment that keeps the air cleanliness at ISO 14644-1 Class 7 or better at all times. It is unnecessary for ordinary dispensing, but if you will serve it in future, allowing room for it at the property stage avoids rework.

**Q. Who applies for the pharmacy permit, and when? Before the property contract?**
A. The pharmacy permit (PMD Act Article 4, paragraph 1) is granted after the structure and equipment are in place and inspected by the health centre. So the order is: property contract → fit-out → permit application → health-centre inspection and examination → opening. The property contract is a precondition of the procedure. Preparation of the permit application and other documents submitted to public offices is undertaken by an administrative scrivener.
