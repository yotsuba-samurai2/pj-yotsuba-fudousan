## Why is the former site of a factory or dry cleaner at higher risk of soil contamination?

The "designated hazardous substances" under the Soil Contamination Countermeasures Act are substances such as lead, arsenic and trichloroethylene, designated by Cabinet Order (Art. 1 of the Enforcement Order) as those that may cause harm to human health through their presence in soil (Art. 2(1) of the Act). Dry cleaners have historically used chlorinated organic solvents such as tetrachloroethylene in dry cleaning, and factories have handled heavy metals and solvents in plating and metalworking; the former sites of facilities that manufactured, used or treated such substances are categorically more likely to retain designated hazardous substances in the soil.

What the buyer should grasp first is not contamination itself but "whether this is land on which a statutory investigation duty arises" and "whether it has received an area designation." These can be confirmed through brokerage and the important-matters explanation. By contrast, actually sampling and measuring the soil's contamination is the work of a designated investigation body designated by the Minister of the Environment or the prefectural governor — not the broker's field. Because the presence and degree of contamination are determined by the result of the on-site investigation, this article does not assert them (unverified).

| Entry point of risk | Buyer's check | Whom to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Past use | History as a specified facility using hazardous substances | Prefecture (registry) / seller |
| Statutory area designation | Area Requiring Action or Requiring Notification | Prefecture / municipality (ledger) |
| Actual soil condition | Concentration of designated hazardous substances | Designated investigation body |

## When does the investigation duty under the Act arise?

The main occasions on which a soil investigation duty (or notification) arises are three. First, when use of a specified facility using hazardous substances (a specified facility under the Water Pollution Prevention Act that handles designated hazardous substances) is discontinued: the owner and the like of the land that was the site of that factory or business place has a designated investigation body investigate and reports the result to the prefectural governor (Art. 3(1) of the Act). However, where the governor confirms, in light of the planned use of the land, that there is no risk of harm to health, the report is deferred (the proviso to the same paragraph).

Second, when changing the character of land on a certain scale or larger. Where the area of the change of character reaches the scale prescribed by Order of the Environment (in principle 3,000 square metres or more; 900 square metres or more for the site of a factory or business place where a specified facility using hazardous substances is currently installed, and the like), a notification must be made to the prefectural governor by 30 days before starting (Art. 4(1)). If the governor finds a risk of contamination, an investigation is ordered (Art. 4(3)). Third, the governor's investigation order for land found to risk harm to health (Art. 5(1)). A buyer who demolishes and develops after acquiring a former site readily falls under the Art. 4 change of character, so confirm the area and timing before acquisition.

| Occasion of the duty | Basis | Meaning for the buyer |
|---|---|---|
| Discontinuation of a specified facility using hazardous substances | Art. 3(1) of the Act | Check the investigation/report history at acquisition |
| Change of character on a certain scale | Art. 4(1) (3,000 m² / 900 m²); Enforcement Regulation | Notify 30 days before demolition/development |
| Governor's investigation order | Art. 5(1) | Land at risk of harm to health |

## How does an area designation as Area Requiring Action or Requiring Notification affect the sale?

Where, as a result of the investigation, the contamination state does not conform to the standards and there is found to be a risk of harm to health, the governor designates the area as an "Area Requiring Action" (Art. 6(1)). In an Area Requiring Action, the governor directs removal and other measures (Art. 7), and in principle a change of the land's character is restricted. On the other hand, land that is non-conforming but carries no risk of harm to health is designated an "Area Requiring Notification for Changes of Character" (Art. 11(1)); to change the character within this area, notification is required by 14 days before starting (Art. 12(1)).

Whether an area is designated can be confirmed in the ledger kept by the prefecture and municipality. Designated land incurs the burden of removal measures or notification and constraints on the buyer's use plan, and may affect price, financing and the handover terms (the degree is individual; we do not assert it, unverified). In the buyer's DD, confirm in the ledger whether the target land falls under an Area Requiring Action or Requiring Notification, and whether there is a past history of designation or release; where designated, organise the content of measures and the cost burden separately with a tax accountant, lawyer and designated investigation body.

| Type of area | Basis of designation | Main regulation |
|---|---|---|
| Area Requiring Action | Art. 6(1) | Direction of removal measures (Art. 7); restriction on changes of character |
| Area Requiring Notification for Changes of Character | Art. 11(1) | Change of character requires notification 14 days before starting (Art. 12(1)) |
| How to confirm the area | Prefecture / municipality ledger | Inquire into the history of designation and release |

## What is the rough cost and time from investigation to measures?

Soil investigation proceeds in stages: a history investigation (document survey) of past use, a preliminary investigation measuring soil and gas for each designated hazardous substance, and a detailed investigation narrowing the contamination range. Cost and time vary greatly with site area, the kinds and number of target substances, and the presence and range of contamination, so no uniform figure can be given (unverified). Even a preliminary investigation accumulates cost in proportion to the number of substances and sections, and proceeding to measures (excavation and removal, in-situ purification, capping and the like) can change the order of magnitude. Confirm the estimate of cost with the designated investigation body.

At the contract stage, it is important for the buyer to set by special clause "who bears the cost of investigation and measures" and "the treatment of non-conformity liability, cancellation and price reduction if contamination is found." Organising the contract clauses is handled by the broker within the important-matters explanation and contract, but disputes over contamination, non-conformity liability, and the tax treatment of removal cost are the fields of a lawyer and a tax accountant respectively. While the cost estimate is unreadable, also consider a structure that holds the handover and settlement terms until the investigation result is out.

| Stage | Content | Who is mainly in charge |
|---|---|---|
| History investigation | Past use and facility history | Designated investigation body / seller materials |
| Preliminary and detailed investigation | Concentration and range of designated hazardous substances | Designated investigation body |
| Measures | Excavation and removal, in-situ purification, capping, etc. | Remediation contractor |
| Contractual arrangement | Cost burden; non-conformity clause | Broker (important-matters explanation, contract) |

## Frequently asked questions

**Q. Where can I find out whether a former site is subject to the Act's investigation duty?**
A. The investigation duty or notification arises when use of a specified facility using hazardous substances is discontinued (Art. 3(1)), or when changing the character of land on a certain scale (Art. 4(1); in principle 3,000 m² or more, and 900 m² or more for the site of such a facility). Confirm the facility's registration history and the area and timing of the change of character with the prefecture and the seller's materials.

**Q. If the land is designated an Area Requiring Action or Requiring Notification, must I not buy?**
A. You are not immediately barred. But an Area Requiring Action (Art. 6(1)) carries a direction of removal measures (Art. 7) and restrictions on changes of character, and an Area Requiring Notification for Changes of Character (Art. 11(1)) requires notification 14 days before starting a change of character (Art. 12(1)). Confirm the designation in the prefecture's or municipality's ledger, and arrange the content of measures and the cost burden separately with a designated investigation body, tax accountant and lawyer.

**Q. Will the broker investigate whether the soil is contaminated?**
A. No. What brokerage and the important-matters explanation can handle is limited to restrictions under laws — whether it is an Area Requiring Action or Requiring Notification (Real Estate Brokerage Act Art. 35(1)(ii)). The soil investigation that samples the soil and measures the concentration of designated hazardous substances is the work of an investigation body designated by the Minister of the Environment or the prefectural governor, to whom we connect you as an independent entity engaged separately. No referral fees are exchanged.

**Q. Who bears the cost of investigation and measures, the seller or the buyer?**
A. The law does not fix it uniformly; it is decided by special clause in the sale contract. Organising the contract clauses — including the treatment of non-conformity liability, cancellation and price reduction if contamination is found — is handled by the broker within the important-matters explanation and contract. Damages and disputes over the cost burden lie with a lawyer, and the tax treatment of removal cost with a tax accountant. While the cost estimate is unreadable, also consider a structure that holds the settlement terms until the investigation result is out.
