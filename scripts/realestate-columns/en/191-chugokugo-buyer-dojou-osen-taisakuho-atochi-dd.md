## Why is the former site of a factory or dry cleaner at higher risk of soil contamination?

The "designated hazardous substances" under the Soil Contamination Countermeasures Act are substances such as lead, arsenic and trichloroethylene, designated by Cabinet Order (Art. 1 of the Enforcement Order) as those that may cause harm to human health through their presence in soil (Art. 2(1) of the Act). Dry cleaners have historically used chlorinated organic solvents such as tetrachloroethylene in dry cleaning, and factories have handled heavy metals and solvents in plating and metalworking; the former sites of facilities that manufactured, used or treated such substances are categorically more likely to retain designated hazardous substances in the soil.

Check the designation register against the seller’s records of facility operation and closure and any previous investigation reports. A site that is not listed is not the same as one that was investigated with negative findings. A designated investigation body carries out the statutory field investigation and evaluates the samples.

| Entry point of risk | Buyer's check | Whom to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Past use | History as a specified facility using hazardous substances | Prefecture (registry) / seller |
| Statutory area designation | Area Requiring Action or Requiring Notification | Prefecture / municipality (ledger) |
| Actual soil condition | Concentration of designated hazardous substances | Designated investigation body |

## When does the investigation duty under the Act arise?

When use of a legally specified facility using hazardous substances ends, the owner or other responsible party must generally commission a designated investigator and report the results to the competent authority (Soil Contamination Countermeasures Act, Art. 3(1)). The authority may confirm that, under the proposed use, there is no risk to human health; investigation is then deferred while those conditions hold. For qualifying works affecting at least 900 m² on land with that confirmation, the owner or other responsible party must notify the authority **in advance** and arrange an investigation and report as ordered (Art. 3(7)–(8)). This is not the separate 30-day procedure below.

Article 4 generally requires advance notice for an alteration of 3,000 m² or more. The threshold is 900 m² on factory or business premises with a specified facility using hazardous substances, and on former premises of such a facility where neither the Article 3 investigation report has been filed nor a deferral has been confirmed (Enforcement Regulation, Article 22). For covered work the person undertaking it gives at least 30 days’ notice. Notice does not in itself trigger an investigation: if the statutory criteria for possible contamination are met, the authority may direct the owner or other statutory landholder to have the land investigated (Article 4(1), (3)). Article 5 separately permits an investigation order where the statutory health-risk criteria are met.

| Situation | Legal basis | Procedure to check |
|---|---|---|
| Discontinuation of a specified facility using hazardous substances | Art. 3(1) of the Act | Check the investigation/report history at acquisition |
| Qualifying works of at least 900 m² on land with an Art. 3 deferral | Art. 3(7)–(8) | Advance notification by the owner or other responsible party; investigation and report as ordered |
| Other qualifying changes to land | Art. 4(1), (3); Regulation Art. 22 | Generally 3,000 m² or more; 900 m² or more at specified sites. Notify at least 30 days before work; an investigation order is separate |
| Governor's investigation order | Art. 5(1) | Land at risk of harm to health |

## How does an area designation as Area Requiring Action or Requiring Notification affect the sale?

Land that exceeds the contamination standard and presents a risk to health is designated an Area Requiring Action (Article 6). The prefectural governor or other competent authority identifies the measures required and the reasons for them, and directs the recipient to submit a response plan (Article 7). Alterations to the land are generally prohibited (Article 9). If the standard is exceeded but the statutory health risk is not found, the land is designated an Area Requiring Notification for Changes of Character (Article 11). A person planning work covered by Article 12 must generally give notice at least 14 days before starting. Specified routine maintenance and minor works are among the exceptions; emergency works after a disaster, for example, must be reported afterwards.

Match the lot number and designated area against the competent authority’s register. A designation may cover only part of the property: compare its map with the planned works. Once the affected area and restrictions are clear, evaluate their impact on the timetable and financing.

| Type of area | Basis of designation | Main regulation |
|---|---|---|
| Area Requiring Action | Article 6(1) | Direction to submit a response plan (Article 7); land alterations generally prohibited (Article 9) |
| Area Requiring Notification for Changes of Character | Article 11(1) | For covered work, generally give notice at least 14 days in advance; statutory exceptions apply (Article 12) |
| How to confirm the area | Prefecture / municipality ledger | Inquire into the history of designation and release |

## How do I get estimates for investigations, remedial work and timing?

For a useful estimate, compile the records of past uses and identify the substances and areas to be investigated. Ask for separate costs for field sampling and analysis and for any remedial works that may prove necessary. Ask when a report will be available, not only when drilling can start.

Before signing, agree who will pay for testing and for any remedial work. Separately, decide what happens to the price, closing or handover if the results affect the proposed use. These contractual terms do not change who receives a direction from the authority. Consult a lawyer about disputed liability and a tax accountant about the tax treatment.

| Stage | Content | Who is mainly in charge |
|---|---|---|
| History investigation | Past use and facility history | Designated investigation body / seller materials |
| Preliminary and detailed investigation | Concentration and range of designated hazardous substances | Designated investigation body |
| Measures | Excavation and removal, in-situ purification, capping, etc. | Remediation contractor |
| Contractual arrangement | Cost burden; non-conformity clause | Broker (important-matters explanation, contract) |

## Frequently asked questions

**Q. Where can I find out whether a former site is subject to the Act's investigation duty?**
A. Ask the seller for the facility's operating and closure records and any Article 3 report or written deferral. Show the planned works to the local authority. Qualifying works of at least 900 m² on land with a confirmed deferral require advance notice under Article 3. For Article 4 works, notice is due at least 30 days before starting; the threshold is generally 3,000 m², or 900 m² at specified sites. Neither a sale nor the site area alone decides whether an investigation is required.

**Q. If the land is designated an Area Requiring Action or Requiring Notification, must I not buy?**
A. Designation alone does not prohibit a sale. For an Area Requiring Action, check the response-plan direction and restrictions on building works. For an Area Requiring Notification for Changes of Character, check whether your project needs at least 14 days’ advance notice or falls within a statutory exception. Compare the authority’s map with the proposed works before budgeting for time and cost.

**Q. Will the broker investigate whether the soil is contaminated?**
A. The broker checks the land’s designation and explains relevant information, such as past investigation reports provided by the seller. This is different from sampling and analysing soil. A statutory on-site investigation must be handled by a designated investigation body.

**Q. Who bears the cost of investigation and measures, the seller or the buyer?**
A. The sale contract can allocate investigation and remedial costs between seller and buyer. It cannot change who must submit a plan under Article 7: normally the owner or other statutory landholder. If another polluter is identified, it is appropriate for that party to act and the landholder does not object, the direction goes to the polluter. Under Article 8, a landholder who was directed to act and carried out the measures may recover qualifying costs of preparing or amending the plan and implementing the measures from another responsible polluter, up to the statutory cap. This is not a general right to recover pre-purchase investigation costs.
