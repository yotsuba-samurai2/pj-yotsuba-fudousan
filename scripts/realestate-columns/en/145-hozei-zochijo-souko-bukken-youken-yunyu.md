**The key point**: A "bonded warehouse" (hozei-zochijo), where imported foreign goods can sit in a duty-unpaid state, is a place licensed by the Director-General of Customs under Customs Act (Act No. 61 of 1954) art. 42 (1). For a warehouse property, three things matter: (1) location based on distance from the customs office (customs practice treats a route of roughly within 25 km of the competent customs office as the guide), (2) a structure and equipment that keep goods safely and prevent wrongdoing (locking, security), and (3) not falling within the grounds for refusal in art. 43 (which include an unsuitable location or facilities). The licence may be renewed for a period not exceeding ten years (art. 42 (2)), and foreign goods may sit duty-unpaid for two years from the day storage was first approved, extendable by the Director-General of Customs for special reasons (art. 43-2). ## How do a bonded warehouse, a "bonded storehouse," and a warehousing registration differ?

The first thing to grasp is that "bonded" and "warehousing business" are separate systems. A bonded warehouse is a customs licence under the Customs Act—a place where foreign goods can sit duty-unpaid. A warehousing registration is a registration with the Minister of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism under the Warehousing Business Act—the qualification for the business of taking others' goods into custody and storing them. The two differ in governing law and jurisdiction, and you may hold both if needed.

Under the Customs Act, a "bonded area" has five types, and a bonded warehouse is one of them. Each type differs in the goods and period it can hold and in the licence/designation mechanism.

| Type of bonded area | Governing article | Character |
|---|---|---|
| Designated bonded area | Customs Act art. 37 | A public facility designated by the Minister of Finance; for short loading/unloading and temporary storage |
| Bonded warehouse | Customs Act art. 42 | Licensed by the Director-General of Customs; allows long-term storage of foreign goods |
| Bonded factory | Customs Act art. 56 | Licensed by the Director-General of Customs; processing/manufacturing of foreign goods while bonded |
| Bonded exhibition site | Customs Act art. 62-2 | Licensed by the Director-General of Customs; exhibiting foreign goods at fairs etc. |
| Integrated bonded area | Customs Act art. 62-8 | An area where storage, processing, exhibition and the like can be done together |

The term "bonded storehouse" is often used colloquially to mean a bonded warehouse. Meanwhile, Warehousing Business Act art. 3 provides that "a person intending to run a warehousing business must receive the registration conducted by the Minister of Land, Infrastructure, Transport and Tourism," and art. 6 provides that registration is refused where the warehouse's facilities/equipment do not meet the ministerial standards for each type. The property requirements for a warehousing registration are covered in [the property requirements for registering a commercial warehouse (Japanese)](https://luck428.com/en/column/eigyo-souko-touroku-bukken-youken). If you only store your own goods, a warehousing registration is not required; if you take others' goods into custody and store them, a separate warehousing registration is required. Having a bonded-warehouse licence does not double as a warehousing registration.

## What location and structure does customs look at for the bonded-warehouse licence?

The bonded-warehouse licence examines not only the applicant (the person) but also the place and the equipment. Customs is generally said to examine from the viewpoints of the personal requirement (the applicant's suitability), the locational requirement (distance from the customs office, etc.), the facility requirement (security, locking, etc.), and the quantitative requirement (the expected volume of goods handled). What matters on the property side is the locational and facility requirements.

Customs Act art. 42 (1) defines a bonded warehouse as "a place that the Director-General of Customs has licensed as one where foreign goods may be loaded/unloaded or carried, or may be placed." As to that licence, art. 43 lists in its items the cases in which the Director-General of Customs may refuse a licence (grounds for refusal), and among them is a ground corresponding to "the location or equipment being found unsuitable for a bonded warehouse." Location and structure take effect here as the suitability of the property.

| Viewpoint | What customs looks at (practice) |
|---|---|
| Locational requirement (location) | A route of roughly within 25 km of the competent customs office is the guide in principle. Even beyond that, roughly within 100 km, there may be room to allow it if roads, ports, airports and other transport facilities are in place |
| Facility requirement (structure/security) | Because foreign goods sit duty-unpaid, a structure/equipment—locking, partitioning, entry control—that prevents loss of goods and wrongdoing |
| Quantitative requirement | Whether the facility's scale matches the expected volume of goods handled |
| Other laws / use | Whether the Building Standards Act zoning allows a (commercial) warehouse, and whether fire and other regulations for the goods can be met |

The locational figures (25 km, 100 km) are guides based on customs practice (such as basic circulars) and are not numbers written in the Act itself. There are exceptional treatments for facilities that do not fit the distance standard, such as those inside a coastal factory loading/unloading directly to and from a ship. The final judgment on this rests with the competent customs office. ## For how long can you keep goods without paying duty?

The biggest advantage of a bonded warehouse is that you can keep foreign goods for a long time before paying customs duty and consumption tax. Customs Act art. 43-2 sets the period foreign goods may sit in a bonded warehouse at two years from the day the goods were first approved to be placed there, and provides that, where there are special reasons, the Director-General of Customs may, on application, designate a necessary period and extend it.

The premise here is "storage approval." Customs Act art. 43-3 (1) provides that, where you intend to keep foreign goods beyond three months from the day they were brought into the bonded warehouse, you must apply to the Director-General of Customs and receive approval before that three months passes. In short: up to three months after bringing them in, you may keep them without approval; to keep them longer you receive approval (storage approval), and long-term storage of two years from the approval day is allowed.

| Period | Content | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Up to three months from the day brought in | May be kept without storage approval | Customs Act art. 43-3 (1) |
| Two years after approval (storage approval) | Long-term storage of foreign goods duty-unpaid | Customs Act art. 43-2 (1) |
| Extension beyond two years | For special reasons, the Director-General of Customs designates a period and extends | Customs Act art. 43-2 (2) |
| For a designated bonded area | In principle one month (shorter than a bonded warehouse) | Under Customs Act art. 36 |

This mechanism of "deferring payment of customs duty/consumption tax while the goods sit" is meaningful for cash flow for operators who want to stagger the timing of import and domestic sale. However, which goods to treat as imported (taken out) and when, and when to pay customs duty/consumption tax, are customs/tax judgments and not a field we advise on. What can be confirmed on the property side is a storage environment that withstands long-term storage (temperature, humidity, partitioning) and whether the structure fits the bonded standards.

## Frequently asked questions

**Q. Do I need a bonded-warehouse licence just to store my own imported goods?**
A. A bonded-warehouse licence (Customs Act art. 42) matters when you want to keep foreign goods for a long time before paying customs duty and consumption tax. Unless it is a licensed place, you cannot, in principle, store foreign goods duty-unpaid. The business of taking others' goods into custody and storing them separately requires a Warehousing Business Act registration (art. 3), and a bonded-warehouse licence does not double as a warehousing registration. What you need changes depending on whether it is bonded storage of your own goods or a storage business for others' goods.

**Q. How close to customs does the property have to be to get a licence?**
A. Customs practice treats a route of roughly within 25 km of the competent customs office as the guide in principle, and even beyond that, roughly within 100 km, there may be room to allow it if roads, ports, airports and other transport facilities are in place. But these are guides based on customs practice (such as basic circulars), not numbers in the Act itself, and there are exceptional treatments for facilities such as those inside a coastal factory. The judgment on distance rests with the competent customs office.

**Q. About how many years can goods sit without paying duty?**
A. Customs Act art. 43-2 (1) sets the period foreign goods may sit in a bonded warehouse at two years from the day storage was first approved (storage approval). For special reasons, the Director-General of Customs may, on application, designate a necessary period and extend it (art. 43-2 (2)). Note that to keep goods beyond three months from the day brought in, you must receive storage approval in advance (art. 43-3 (1)). When to treat goods as imported (taken out) and pay customs duty/consumption tax is a customs/tax judgment.

**Q. Will a real-estate company do the bonded-warehouse licence application for me?**
A. No. Supporting the bonded-warehouse licence application is the field of a customs broker/administrative scrivener, and import/export customs clearance is the field of a registered customs specialist; we do not do them. What we handle is the check of the warehouse property's zoning, structure and location, and the lease/sale brokerage. This article is general information and does not guarantee that a particular property can obtain a licence, so confirm the prospects with the competent customs office and bonded/customs professionals.
